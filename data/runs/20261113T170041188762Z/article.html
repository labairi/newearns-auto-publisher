The first step in working out how to make money on Instagram is knowing who actually pays you. Instagram pays creators directly only through Bonuses, which its own page describes as limited time and invite-only. Every other option is money from fans buying inside the app, from brands, or from your own customers. The in-app tools have eligibility rules that, as Buffer notes, may vary by region and over time.

The four ways money reaches you on Instagram

Instagram's creator site says "There's no one-size-fits-all approach to making money on Instagram" and suggests you can use Subscriptions, Gifts and brand partnerships at the same time. Sorting the options by who pays makes it easier to see which ones fit your account today:

  1. Your followers, inside Instagram: Subscriptions, Gifts on Reels and Badges during Live.
  2. Instagram itself: Bonuses, by invitation only.
  3. Brands: sponsored posts, partnership ads, affiliate links and user-generated content (UGC).
  4. Your own customers: products and services you sell, with Instagram as the place people find you.

If you are comparing Instagram with other channels, our guide to how to make money online and what each platform takes covers the wider picture.

Getting paid by your followers inside Instagram

Subscriptions

According to Instagram's Subscriptions page, subscribers pay a monthly fee to see exclusive content highlighted with a purple ring, and they get a subscriber badge next to their name in comments and DMs. You set the price, and you can change it later in the subscription settings. Wix reports that the price can be set anywhere from $0.99 to $99.99 a month; check the options your own app shows.

Payouts arrive on the 21st of the following month. Instagram's example: earnings from March are paid on April 21 (source). That gap matters if you are counting on the money for bills.

Before you switch Subscriptions on, write down what subscribers will get each week, for example a weekly breakdown post, a monthly tutorial or early access to content you would otherwise post publicly. People are paying every month, so plan exclusive content you can keep producing.

Gifts on Reels

Instagram's Gifts page says people can purchase Stars and share virtual gifts in the app to show appreciation for Reels they love. That page doesn't list eligibility criteria or a payout rate. Buffer reports that Gifts are offered to creators with a professional account and at least 500 followers, in selected countries only.

Because Gifts are tied to Reels, they only make sense if you publish Reels regularly. Our guide to the best time to post on Instagram shows how to find the posting times that suit your audience.

Badges during Live

Instagram's Badges page says viewers can buy badges during your Live in $0.99, $1.99 and $4.99 increments, up to USD $250 per live video. You get paid once you've earned at least $100, and the money then takes 1–7 business days to arrive, depending on your bank.

On fees, the same page says: "Google and Apple deduct 30% of all in-app purchases in fees. Currently Instagram receives no portion of the fees." That statement appears on the Badges page only, and the word "currently" means it can change. Don't assume the same split applies to Subscriptions or Gifts; check the earnings details in your own account.

Eligibility rules that apply to all three

Instagram's own Subscriptions and Gifts pages don't list who qualifies, so the thresholds in this guide come from secondary sources. For Subscriptions, Buffer lists these requirements (source):

  • Be at least 18.
  • Have a professional Instagram account with at least 10K followers.
  • Live in a country where the feature is available.
  • Have a profile that meets Instagram's Content Monetization and Partner Monetization policies.
  • Not mainly post content focused on children; Buffer says Subscriptions aren't available to those accounts.

Wix agrees on a Professional account, at least 10,000 followers and a supported country. Buffer adds that these thresholds may vary by region and over time. Instagram's menus also differ by country, app version and account type, so treat what your own app shows as the final word. If a feature doesn't appear for you, it isn't available to your account yet.

Bonuses: when Instagram pays you directly

Instagram says Bonuses reward the performance of your reels and photos, including carousels and single image posts, and that they are "limited time and invite-only as we build toward a sustainable program" (source). Wix also describes them as currently invite-only.

You can't apply. If you are invited, Buffer notes that you must add a payout account to receive a bonus payout. Instagram doesn't publish rates in the sources we read, so treat any Bonus as extra money, not as income you plan around.

Getting paid by brands

Sponsored posts and how to price them

Instagram says you'll negotiate the terms of every branded content deal, so each one will be different. It suggests giving the brand data such as your number of followers and your average likes, views, comments and shares (source). Put that into a one-page media kit:

  1. Who you are: handle, niche and the kind of content you make.
  2. Your numbers: followers, plus average likes, views, comments and shares across recent posts. Use averages rather than your single best post.
  3. Where your audience is: if a brand only sells in certain countries, show where your followers are. Our guide to Instagram follower location explains how to find this.
  4. What you offer: list deliverables separately, for example one Reel, three Stories, or a carousel.
  5. Usage terms: say whether the brand can run your post as an ad, and price that separately (see partnership ads below).

We don't quote per-post rates here, because we couldn't find a source we could verify. Build your quote from your deliverables and your data, and adjust after each deal.

Finding brands

There are three main routes:

  • Instagram's Creator Marketplace. Instagram presents it as a way for creators to get discovered by brands. Wix reports that to join, creators must be 18 or older and follow Instagram's Partner Monetization Policies, Community Guidelines and Content Monetization Policies.
  • Pitching directly. Start with brands you already buy and mention. Send a short message with one specific content idea, your deliverables and your media kit numbers.
  • Other matchmaking marketplaces. These let you create a profile and receive or send collaboration requests outside Instagram.

One option for the last route is Instagramaps. It includes a marketplace where creators and businesses create profiles, get matched and send collaboration requests. It also lets you connect an Instagram account to see where your audience engages by country and city, and compares views, reach and engagement per post, which is the kind of data Instagram suggests you bring to a negotiation. It has free Creator and Business plans, and paid plans start at 9 USD per month (check the pricing page for current prices).

Partnership ads: ask for more when a brand runs your post as an ad

With partnership ads, brands run your content as an ad to reach people beyond your followers. Instagram says that because the brand puts money behind your content, "you can advocate for higher pay given the extended usage rights" (source).

  • Reels, stories, static posts and carousels can all be run as partnership ads.
  • Content that includes licensed music can't be run as a partnership ad.
  • You need to give ad permissions before the brand can promote your post.
  • If you post first and the brand boosts it, the post shows on your grid. If you send the content to the brand to upload through Ads Manager, it doesn't.

In practice: ask early whether the brand plans to run your content as an ad, quote ad usage as a separate line, and agree whether the post should appear on your grid.

Affiliate links and UGC

Buffer notes that brand deals, UGC work and affiliate marketing have no minimum follower count, which makes them the usual starting point for smaller accounts.

  • Affiliate marketing: you join a brand's or retailer's affiliate program, share a tracked link or code, and earn a commission when people buy. Commission rates and payout rules are set by each program, so read its terms before you promote.
  • UGC: a brand pays you to make photos or videos that it posts on its own channels or uses in its ads. Your follower count matters less than the quality of the content, so a small portfolio of sample videos does most of the selling.

Selling your own products and services

Your own offer doesn't depend on Instagram's monetization rules. Common options include courses, templates, coaching, freelance services such as editing or photography, and merch. In this model Instagram is the shop window: you show your work in posts, Reels and Stories, then send people to wherever you take payment, such as your own website or a third-party store. Each store or payment platform sets its own fees, so check their pricing pages.

  1. Pick one offer that matches what people already ask you about in comments or DMs.
  2. Set up checkout on a platform you control.
  3. Post content that shows the result your product delivers, and point to the purchase link.
  4. Answer questions in DMs and turn the common ones into posts.

Disclosing paid posts: Instagram's label and FTC rules

Instagram's rules. Instagram says: "If you're getting paid in any form for your content, you'll need to add a Paid Partnership label." It also says free trips and gifted products count: "anything received from business partners counts, whether it be money or otherwise" (source). Some products, like drugs and weapons, can't be promoted as branded content. Others, like alcohol, can only be promoted with restrictions. You also shouldn't put pre-, mid- or post-roll ads in videos or audio, or banner ads in videos or images.

FTC rules (U.S.). The FTC's Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers says:

  • You must disclose a "material connection" to a brand. That includes personal, family, employment or financial relationships, "such as the brand paying you or giving you free or discounted products or services."
  • Disclosure is your responsibility as the influencer.
  • If a brand gives you free or discounted products or other perks and you then mention one of its products, disclose it even if you weren't asked to mention that product.
  • Disclosures are likely to be missed if they appear only on a profile page, at the end of posts or videos, or behind a "more" click. Don't mix them into a group of hashtags or links.
  • On Instagram Stories, superimpose the disclosure over the picture. In a live stream, repeat it periodically.
  • "Advertisement," "ad" and "sponsored" are clear terms. Avoid "sp," "spon," "collab," and stand-alone terms like "thanks" or "ambassador."
  • Don't assume a platform's disclosure tool is good enough on its own; use it in addition to your own disclosure.
  • If you post from abroad, U.S. law applies if it's reasonably foreseeable that the post will affect U.S. consumers, and foreign laws might also apply.

The FTC also says that if you have no brand relationship and are just telling people about a product you bought and like, you don't need to declare that. A simple routine for paid posts: turn on the Paid Partnership label, start the caption with "Ad" before the "more" cut-off, add the word on screen in Stories and Reels, and repeat it during sponsored Lives.

Taxes on Instagram income

For U.S. readers, the IRS says you must report gig economy income on a tax return even if it comes from part-time or side work, isn't reported on a form like a 1099-K, 1099-MISC, 1099-NEC or W-2, or is paid in any form, including cash, property, goods or virtual currency (IRS Gig Economy Tax Center). Its examples of gig work include "Sell goods online" and "Provide creative or professional services," and it notes the list isn't complete.

Keep records of every payout and of the value of gifted products. If you live elsewhere, check your own tax authority's rules. For how income types are classified, see passive income vs active income.

Which path to start with

Use the facts above to choose where to put your effort first:

  • Below the reported thresholds: start with UGC, affiliate links, direct brand pitches and your own product or service, since Buffer reports no minimum for brand deals, UGC and affiliate work.
  • Past 500 followers and making Reels: check whether Gifts appear in your app.
  • Past 10K followers in a supported country, with content you can keep producing: look at Subscriptions, and remember payouts come on the 21st of the following month.
  • Comfortable going live: test Badges, keeping in mind the $100 payout threshold.
  • Already doing brand deals: add partnership ad pricing to your rate sheet.

Whatever your size, the most dependable answer to how to make money on Instagram is to combine more than one method, as Instagram itself suggests, rather than wait for an invitation to Bonuses.

FAQ

Does Instagram pay for views?

Only through Bonuses, which reward the performance of reels and photos and are limited time and invite-only. Instagram doesn't publish rates in the sources we read.

Do I need a big following to make money on Instagram?

Not for every method. Buffer reports 500 followers for Gifts and 10K for Subscriptions, but no minimum for brand deals, UGC and affiliate marketing. Selling your own products has no Instagram threshold at all. Check the in-app tools in your own account, because requirements can vary by region and over time.

When do Subscriptions pay out?

Instagram says on the 21st of the following month; March earnings, for example, are paid on April 21.

Do gifted products need a Paid Partnership label?

Yes. Instagram says anything received from business partners counts, whether it be money or otherwise, and the FTC treats free or discounted products as a material connection you must disclose.

Do I owe tax if I didn't get a 1099?

In the U.S., yes. The IRS says gig income must be reported even if it isn't on a 1099 or W-2, and even if it's paid in goods or virtual currency.
