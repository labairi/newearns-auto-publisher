Most free AI tools are really free plans with usage limits. They are not unlimited products, and they are not short trials. This guide uses each vendor's own pricing and help pages to show what the free plans from ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Microsoft Copilot and Canva include and where they stop. It also covers three things to check before free AI output goes into work a client pays for.

Free AI tools for writing and everyday work

ChatGPT Free

OpenAI's pricing page lists the Free plan as limited access to its current default model, plus limited messages and uploads, limited and slower image generation, limited deep research, limited memory and context, and limited Codex access. In practice, the free plan works for drafting a proposal, rewriting an email or outlining a blog post. You can expect to hit a ceiling during a long working session.

The next tier, Go, is described as "Everything in Free" plus more access, messages, uploads and image creation, and longer memory. The page also says "This plan may include ads." Plus adds advanced reasoning, expanded deep research and agent mode, and Projects, tasks and custom GPTs. OpenAI notes that "Limits apply." Model names on this page change often, so check the page itself rather than relying on any article, including this one.

Once you've chosen a tool, these ChatGPT prompts for freelancers and side hustles give you copy-ready starting points for proposals, client emails and content outlines.

Claude Free

Anthropic's pricing page lists Claude Free at $0, "Free for everyone". It covers chat on web, desktop and mobile; searching the web, creating files and running code; memory across conversations; connecting your apps and tools; and creating Artifacts. The ability to create files and run code is useful if you want a quick spreadsheet, a formatted document or a small calculation without leaving the chat.

Pro costs $17 per month with the annual discount ($200 billed up front), or $20 if billed monthly. It adds more usage, Claude Code, Projects and more Claude models. Anthropic notes that prices exclude applicable tax and that "Price and plans are subject to change at Anthropic's discretion."

Microsoft Copilot (free) vs Copilot in Microsoft 365

These are two different products. Microsoft's support page says Microsoft Copilot "is available at no cost at copilot.cloud.microsoft". It is also available as an app and in the Edge browser. The free version lets you chat for help with writing, brainstorming and summarizing, get real-time answers based on web search, summarize webpages in Edge, and create images.

You don't have to sign in. Microsoft says, however, that signing in with a personal account "gives you access to chat history, image creation, longer conversations, voice interactions, and other Copilot features." If images seem to be missing, check that you are signed in.

Copilot inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and OneNote is a separate offer that needs a Microsoft 365 subscription. On the same page, Microsoft states: "Copilot features in Microsoft 365 Personal, Family, Premium, and Pro subscriptions are subject to limits and can be used by subscription owner only." On a Family plan, that means the other members don't get Copilot in the apps. Microsoft also says features and availability "may vary by region."

Gemini Free

Google's plans page lists the free tier as the Gemini app with access to its Flash-Lite model, image generation and editing, Gems (custom assistants), access to the Google Flow creative studio, and 15 GB of storage.

Gemini's limits work differently from a message count. According to the page's footnote, "compute-based usage limits factor in the complexity of your prompt, the features you use, and the length of your chat. Your limit refreshes every 5 hours until you reach your weekly limit." The page also says you can buy AI credits to extend your limits. In practice, one long chat with heavy features can use more of your allowance than several short questions. If you run out mid-task, starting a new, shorter chat for the next task may use less of your allowance. That follows from the footnote's mention of chat length, not from a published figure. Google also notes that "Rate limits may apply."

Free AI tools for images and design

Several free plans include some image generation:

  • ChatGPT Free: "Limited and slower image generation", according to OpenAI's pricing page.
  • Gemini Free: image generation and editing, plus Google Flow access "including limited access to Nano Banana Pro", per Google's plans page.
  • Microsoft Copilot (free): creates images. Signing in with a personal account unlocks image creation, according to Microsoft's support page.

None of these pages gives an exact number of free images, so plan around "some" rather than a fixed quota. Canva is the exception, and it documents its allowance in the most detail.

Canva Free: how the 20 monthly AI uses are counted

According to Canva's help page on AI usage, Canva Free includes "Up to 20 uses of Standard AI tools, OR Premium AI tools," and "No access to Ultra AI tools." Here is how that works in practice:

  • Standard tools count on Free. Canva says that "For Canva Free, Standard AI tools still draw from the monthly AI allowance." Its list of Standard tools includes Image Upscaler, image generation with Canva AI and Magic Formulas, and Magic Write appears among its design tools. On paid plans, Standard tools don't draw from the allowance, though fair use limits apply.
  • One generation isn't always one use. Canva calls its ranges "estimates" and says "More complex prompts and tasks use more of your monthly AI allowance." A detailed, multi-part request may use more than a simple one.
  • The reset is in UTC. The allowance "resets at 12:00 a.m. UTC on the first of each month." If you're in the Americas, that falls on the evening of the last day of the previous month, local time.
  • Hitting the limit slows you down. Canva says that once Free users reach the limit, "you'll see short pauses between generations for Standard and Premium AI tools."

Here is a simple way to make 20 uses last a month. Use free, non-AI editing (templates, text, layout) for most of the work. Save AI uses for the steps where AI clearly helps, such as upscaling a low-resolution client logo or generating a background image.

Free AI video: what's actually included

Free plans mostly don't include AI video generation:

  • Google: its plans page lists video generation among the extras in the paid AI Plus ($4.99/month) and AI Pro ($19.99/month) plans. These are US prices.
  • Canva: "Create a video clip with Canva AI" is listed as an Ultra AI tool on Canva's help page. Canva Free has no access to Ultra tools. Pro includes up to 20 Ultra uses.
  • None of the free-plan feature lists for ChatGPT, Claude and Copilot summarized in this guide mention AI video generation.

If you find a tool advertised as a "free AI video generator", check three things on the vendor's own pricing or help page before you build a client project around it: whether free exports carry a watermark, how many credits or clips the free tier allows, and whether the terms permit commercial use.

Before you use free AI output in paid work

Check accuracy

The tool doesn't take responsibility for the output; you do. OpenAI's Terms of Use state: "You must evaluate Output for accuracy and appropriateness for your use case, including using human review as appropriate, before using or sharing Output from the Services." Before you deliver anything, check names, figures, dates, quotes and links against the original source.

Ownership is not the same as copyright

OpenAI's Terms of Use say that, "As between you and OpenAI, and to the extent permitted by applicable law," you retain ownership of your input and "own the Output." The same terms warn that "output may not be unique and other users may receive similar output." That matters if a client expects an exclusive logo or slogan. The linked version is OpenAI's rest-of-world terms, and terms may differ by region. Other vendors have their own terms, so read them for the tool you use.

Owning output under a contract is a different question from whether copyright law protects it. In January 2025, the US Copyright Office concluded that generative AI outputs "can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements." According to its announcement, this can include cases where "a human makes creative arrangements or modifications of the output, but not the mere provision of prompts." It also says that using AI to assist creation, or including AI material in a larger human-made work, "does not bar copyrightability." This is the US position (more on the Office's AI page). Rules elsewhere may differ, and this guide isn't legal advice. If copyright matters for a deliverable, make your own substantial creative changes and tell the client how AI was used.

Keep client data out of free chats, or change your settings

  • ChatGPT: OpenAI's Terms of Use say: "If you do not want us to use your Content to train our models, you can opt out." They add that opting out may in some cases "limit the ability of our Services to better address your specific use case."
  • Gemini: Google's Gemini Apps Privacy Hub asks you not to "enter confidential information that you wouldn't want a reviewer to see or Google to use to improve our services." To stop future chats from being reviewed to improve Google services, turn off the Keep Activity setting. You can also use temporary chats, which Google says "are not used to improve Google AI with help from human reviewers." Even then, Google says it still uses your chats to respond to you and to help protect Google, its users and the public, "including with help from human reviewers."

A safe habit for any tool: replace client names, emails and figures with placeholders such as [CLIENT] or [AMOUNT] before pasting text, then put the real details back in your own document.

How much time can AI save? What one study found

An experiment published in Science and reported by MIT News gave 453 college-educated professionals, including marketers, grant writers, consultants and data analysts, 20- to 30-minute writing tasks related to their jobs. Access to ChatGPT cut the time taken by 40 percent, and output quality, as rated by independent evaluators, rose by 18 percent.

The limits matter, according to the same MIT News report. The tasks "didn't require precise factual accuracy or context about things like a company's goals or a customer's preferences," and the researchers didn't hire fact-checkers. Co-author Shakked Noy said the speed benefits may be "lesser in the real world because you need to spend time fact-checking and writing the prompts." Treat the result as evidence from one 2023 experiment on short writing tasks, not as a promise for your own work.

When a paid plan is worth it

Base the decision on what each vendor says the paid plan adds, and on whether you keep hitting a specific limit:

  • You keep running out of Gemini usage, or you need video: Google says AI Plus ($4.99/month) offers 2x the free usage limits and AI Pro ($19.99/month) 4x, both with video generation (US prices). The page also notes "No refunds for partial billing periods, except as required by applicable law."
  • You need longer working sessions or organized client work in Claude: Claude Pro adds more usage and Projects, at $17/month billed annually or $20 billed monthly, before tax.
  • You use up Canva's 20 uses or need video clips: Canva Pro provides up to 200 Premium or 20 Ultra AI uses, and Standard tools stop counting toward the allowance.
  • You want AI inside Word or Excel: that needs a Microsoft 365 subscription, and only the subscription owner gets Copilot in the apps.
  • You hit ChatGPT Free's message or image limits: compare Go and Plus on OpenAI's pricing page, and note that Go "may include ads."

A practical rule is to stay on free plans until a limit costs you billable time on more than one occasion, then upgrade only the tool causing it. FAQ

Is ChatGPT free?

Yes, there is a free plan, but it comes with limits. OpenAI's pricing page describes limited access to the default model, limited messages and uploads, limited and slower image generation, and limited deep research, memory and Codex access. Paid tiers raise those limits.

Can I sell images made with free AI tools?

It depends on each tool's terms and on your local law. OpenAI's Terms of Use say you own the output, to the extent permitted by law, but that similar output may go to other users. In the US, the Copyright Office says prompts alone don't make output copyrightable. For other tools, read the vendor's own terms before you sell.

Do free AI tools train on my data?

Check each tool's settings. OpenAI's terms describe an opt-out from model training. Google's Gemini privacy hub says human reviewers may see chats, and it explains how the Keep Activity setting and temporary chats change that. For Claude, Copilot and Canva, read their privacy pages. Whichever tool you use, keep confidential client details out of your prompts.
