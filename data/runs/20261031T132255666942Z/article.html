What is an AI agent? In short, it is an AI system you give a goal to, and it carries out several steps on its own using tools such as a browser, your files or connected apps. A chatbot answers one prompt at a time and waits for your next message.

This guide is for freelancers, creators and small business owners who want to know what that means in practice. It draws only on vendor documentation and published research as of October 2026. It covers what the agents in ChatGPT, Claude and Microsoft Copilot are documented to do, which plans include them, how to use them safely, and what benchmarks say about how often they still fail. Agent features are rolling out quickly, so screens and menus may look different from what is described here.

What is an AI agent? A plain-English definition

Google Cloud puts it this way: “AI agents are software systems that use AI to pursue goals and complete tasks on behalf of users.” According to Google Cloud's explainer, agents show reasoning, planning and memory, and have some autonomy to make decisions, learn and adapt.

IBM's overview adds the technical core. Agents are built on large language models (LLMs). They use “tool calling” to fetch up-to-date information, and they break a goal into subtasks. IBM describes non-agent chatbots as working “without available tools, memory or reasoning.”

Chatbot vs AI agent vs bot

Google Cloud separates three things that often get lumped together. The table below follows its comparison, with IBM's description of non-agent chatbots added.

A quick test: if you make every decision and type every next step, you are using an assistant. If the tool does the same fixed thing every time, it is a bot or automation. If you describe an outcome and the tool works out the steps, it is acting as an agent.

How an agent works, in four steps

Google Cloud describes agents in terms of reasoning, acting, observing and planning. Put in everyday order, a task usually goes like this:

  1. Plan. The agent reasons about your goal and breaks it into subtasks, which is how IBM describes agents working toward a goal.
  2. Use tools. It acts, for example by opening web pages, reading files or using a connected app.
  3. Check results. It observes what each action produced and adjusts the next step.
  4. Ask you. It stops when it needs you. OpenAI's cloud browser, for example, pauses for your input, a sign-in or a confirmation.

ChatGPT Work and the cloud browser

According to OpenAI's help center, ChatGPT now includes Chat and Work. “Work is an agent designed for longer, multi-step work and finished deliverables.” Codex is the separate option for software development.

  • Where it runs: Work is available on web and mobile for eligible paid plans, and in the desktop app when your plan includes it. On web and mobile it runs in the cloud, and OpenAI says it “cannot directly access files on your computer.” The desktop app can use local files and apps with your permission, though messages and task context may still be stored in the cloud (OpenAI).
  • Usage: Work follows the same usage structure as Codex, and “Work usage will vary by task” (OpenAI).
  • Tasks triggered by events: Work can start tasks from events, for example from Slack. These are “not available on Free or Go, or in FedRAMP workspaces.” Admins of Enterprise, Edu and Healthcare workspaces must turn them on first (OpenAI).

The cloud browser “gives ChatGPT Work its own browser on a separate computer in the cloud.” It can read pages, click, fill in forms and take steps on public sites and on sites you have signed into. It keeps working after you leave and pauses when it needs your input, a sign-in or a confirmation. It is available on “paid ChatGPT plans in supported regions, excluding Free and Go,” and availability may also vary during rollout and by workspace permissions.

  • Site access: it asks before visiting a new site. In settings you can choose Always ask, Auto approve or Always allow. Of the last option, OpenAI says: “This is not recommended.”
  • Confirmations: it asks before actions that “could be hard to reverse or create a financial, legal, account, or other real-world commitment.”
  • Sign-ins: you log in through a secure form, and the credentials are “not visible to the model.” Sessions persist until they expire. The cloud browser does not use your own browser's cookies or sign-ins.
  • Limits: websites can block automated agents, so a task may not finish. OpenAI notes these restrictions “are set by the website, not by ChatGPT” (OpenAI).

Claude Cowork

Anthropic says Cowork “brings Claude Code's agentic capabilities to knowledge work beyond coding.” You describe an outcome, step away, and come back to documents, organized files or research. It runs in the cloud and includes scheduled tasks and projects.

  • Plans: paid plans only, namely Pro, Max, Ultra, Team and Enterprise. Web and mobile access varies by plan. Team is in beta, and on Enterprise an owner must turn it on.
  • Desktop app: local files, browser use and computer use need the Claude Desktop app (macOS or Windows) open and connected.
  • Approval modes: Manual; Auto, where Claude reviews each action for safety and blocks unsafe ones; and Skip, where “nothing checks its actions automatically.” For work involving “money, messages sent as you, important files,” Anthropic says to stay close or switch back to Manual.
  • Usage: “Multi-step tasks use more of your usage than quick questions” (Anthropic).

Agents in the Microsoft Copilot app

Microsoft's getting-started page describes three kinds of agents in the Microsoft 365 Copilot app: ready-made agents, agents made by your organization, and agents you build yourself. To find them, open the navigation panel, go to Agents, then All agents (Agent Store), and choose Add or Open. Microsoft notes that “Agent capabilities and availability can vary based on your account type, subscription, and organization settings,” so what you see depends on your account. If you use a work account, your IT admin controls much of this.

What AI agents can do for freelancers and small businesses

The vendors publish their own example tasks. Here they are grouped by the kind of work New Earns readers do. None of them come with documented time savings, and each vendor says some tasks need your review.

  • Admin and money: OpenAI lists “Reconcile invoices and update records in your accounting software,” requesting quotes and comparing utility plans as cloud browser tasks (OpenAI). Anthropic lists turning receipts into an expense report (Anthropic).
  • Research and writing: Cowork examples include research synthesis, transcript analysis, reports from voice memos and slide decks from notes (Anthropic).
  • Files and data: organizing a Downloads folder, batch renaming files, cleaning data, making charts, and building Excel files “with working VLOOKUP” (Anthropic).
  • Everyday errands: comparing flights or stock, tracking packages, saving apartment listings and preparing a DMV booking for your approval (OpenAI).
  • Recurring work: Cowork offers scheduled tasks (Anthropic), and ChatGPT Work can run tasks triggered by events on eligible plans (OpenAI).

For example, a freelance designer could ask Cowork to turn a month of receipt files into an expense report, then check the totals against the receipts before sending it to an accountant. OpenAI notes that some cloud browser tasks need your review or a final step you complete yourself.

What AI agents still can't do reliably

Documented limits

  • Websites can say no. Sites can block automated agents, and the task may not finish (OpenAI).
  • Computer use is slow and can misclick. Anthropic's developer docs say delays “might be too slow compared to regular human-directed computer actions,” that Claude can make mistakes with on-screen coordinates, and: “Do not use Claude for tasks requiring perfect precision or sensitive user information without human oversight” (Anthropic).
  • Safeguards are not complete. OpenAI says its protections against prompt injection, phishing and unintended actions “do not eliminate every risk” (OpenAI). Anthropic says “The chances of an attack are still non-zero” (Anthropic).
  • Some work is a poor fit. Google Cloud lists tasks that need deep empathy, carry high ethical stakes or happen in unpredictable physical environments. It also notes that resource-intensive builds may not suit smaller organizations with limited budgets (Google Cloud).
  • Agents can loop or leak. IBM warns of infinite feedback loops, where “some level of real-time human monitoring might be used.” It also flags privacy risks from the “experimental and often unpredictable behavior of agentic AI” (IBM).

What benchmarks show

These three tests measure different things, so their percentages should not be compared with each other.

  • TheAgentCompany (NeurIPS 2025): in a simulated small software company, “with the most competitive agent, 30% of the tasks can be completed autonomously.” Simpler tasks were often solved, but “more difficult long-horizon tasks are still beyond the reach of current systems” (NeurIPS paper).
  • OSWorld (2025): this test measures how well agents use a computer. The Stanford HAI 2026 AI Index shows accuracy rising from about 5% in 2024 to 66.3% in 2025, still below a 72.35% human baseline (Stanford HAI).
  • Remote Labor Index (as of October 2025): on real projects of the kind people are paid to do remotely, “AI agents perform near the floor,” and the best agent reached an automation rate of 2.5% (arXiv). Newer models may score differently.

The practical takeaway is to give agents short, well-defined tasks and to treat what they produce as a draft you check.

How to try your first agent task safely

Exact buttons may differ by plan and while features roll out.

  1. Check your plan. The cloud browser excludes Free and Go (OpenAI), and Cowork is for paid plans only (Anthropic).
  2. Pick a low-stakes task. Research synthesis, comparing utility plans or renaming a copy of a folder are safer starts than anything involving payments or client messages.
  3. Describe the outcome clearly. Say what you want, which sources or folders to use, the format of the result, and what the agent must not do. Our ChatGPT prompts for freelancers and side hustles show how to write clear, specific instructions.
  4. Choose cautious settings. In the cloud browser, keep site access on Always ask. In Cowork, use Manual for anything involving “money, messages sent as you, important files” (Anthropic).
  5. For local files in Cowork, open the Desktop app. It must be open and connected for local files, browser use and computer use.
  6. Sign in only through the secure form. The cloud browser pauses for sign-ins, and those credentials are not visible to the model (OpenAI).
  7. Stay reachable and review. Answer confirmation prompts yourself, then check the result before you use or send it.

Safety checklist

  • Understand prompt injection. Anthropic explains that it needs two things at once: Claude reads content from outside your trusted sources, and it can take actions that could harm you. Limit either one and you cut the risk (Anthropic).
  • Never paste secrets. OpenAI: “Never paste passwords, security codes, or payment details into the conversation” (OpenAI).
  • Keep sensitive work out of agent browsers. Anthropic says “we strongly advise against using either to manage or take actions involving sensitive information” (Anthropic).
  • Know which tools skip checks. In Cowork, computer use clicks and types “without the permission checks that gate other Cowork tools.” Claude does always ask before permanently deleting files, in any mode (Anthropic).
  • Limit access. Connect only the apps and folders a task needs, and work on copies of important files.
  • Avoid “always allow” settings. OpenAI calls Always allow “not recommended,” and Anthropic's Skip mode means nothing checks actions automatically.
  • Stop tasks that go wrong. If an agent heads somewhere you did not intend, stop it rather than letting it continue.

What the research says about AI, agents and jobs

The evidence is early and uneven, and none of it predicts what will happen to any one person's income.

  • Adoption is still early. Citing a McKinsey survey, the 2026 AI Index reports that 88% of surveyed organizations use AI, but scaled agent deployment “was in the single digits across nearly all business functions.” In the technology sector it was higher: software engineering 24%, IT 22% and service operations 21% (AI Index 2026, Economy).
  • Agent skills are showing up in job ads. Job postings referencing agentic AI or AI agents “exponentially increased” from 2024 to 2025, while postings mentioning ChatGPT or chatbots fell as a share (AI Index 2026).
  • Early-career workers in exposed jobs have been hit. Citing Brynjolfsson et al. (2025), the report says that “By September 2025, employment for software developers ages 22–25 had fallen close to 20% from its 2022 peak.” In the most AI-exposed jobs, employment for workers aged 22–25 fell about 16% compared with the least exposed. These figures cover AI exposure in general, not agents specifically, and the report adds that the evidence “does not point to broad, uniform displacement” (AI Index 2026).
  • Productivity results for AI assistants (not agents) vary. Support agents resolved 14–15% more issues per hour (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025), and developers using GitHub Copilot completed 26% more pull requests (Cui et al., 2025). METR found experienced open-source developers were 19% slower with AI, but a later study could not repeat that result. The report calls effects “highly context dependent” (AI Index 2026).
  • Employers' expectations are not outcomes. About one-third of organizations expect AI to reduce their workforce in the coming year. That is a forecast from employers, not a measured result (AI Index 2026).

Watch out for “AI agent” income schemes

“Let an AI agent run your store” is an easy pitch to sell. On 25 September 2024, the FTC announced Operation AI Comply, a set of five enforcement actions over deceptive AI claims and schemes (FTC). The allegations, as announced at the time, included:

  • Ascend Ecom allegedly claimed its AI tools would bring passive income and allegedly “defrauded consumers of at least $25 million.”
  • Ecommerce Empire Builders claimed clients could make $10,000 a month; the FTC alleged the company “has no evidence to back up those claims.”
  • FBA Machine allegedly cost consumers “more than $15.9 million.”

At the time of the announcement, Ascend Ecom and Ecommerce Empire Builders had been halted by court order and those cases were ongoing. Then-Chair Lina M. Khan said “there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books” (FTC). Compare any “AI income” pitch with the benchmark results above, and ask for evidence before you pay. FAQ

Is ChatGPT agent mode still available?

No. OpenAI's help page says “ChatGPT agent is no longer available” and points users to ChatGPT Work, which can use the cloud browser for tasks on websites.

Do AI agents work on free plans?

Not the ones covered here, as of October 2026. The cloud browser is for paid ChatGPT plans in supported regions, excluding Free and Go. Cowork is for paid Claude plans only. Microsoft says agent availability varies by account type, subscription and organization settings.

Can an agent log in to my accounts?

Yes, with conditions. ChatGPT's cloud browser takes logins through a secure form that is not visible to the model, sessions last until they expire, and it does not use your own browser's sign-ins. It also asks for confirmation before actions with financial, legal, account or other real-world commitments (OpenAI).

Are AI agents safe to use with client data?

Be cautious. Anthropic strongly advises against using Cowork's browser features for sensitive information and says the chance of an attack is “still non-zero” (Anthropic). OpenAI says its safeguards “do not eliminate every risk.” Check your client agreements, give the agent only the files a task needs, and review everything before it leaves your hands.
