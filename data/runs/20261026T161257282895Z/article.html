Real data entry jobs from home come in three forms: employee roles where a company pays you a wage, freelance gigs you win on a marketplace, and per-task microwork paid by a platform. The difficulty is that, as the FTC warns, “Scammers advertise jobs the same way honest employers do — online (in ads, on job sites, and social media).”

This guide explains what the work involves, what official data says it pays and who that data covers, where real listings are and what each channel costs you, how today's job scams work, and a short check to run before you reply to any offer. It ends with what to do if you have already paid or shared personal details.

If you are still deciding whether data entry suits you, our roundup of smart ways to make money from home covers other options.

What remote data entry work actually involves

O*NET, the U.S. Department of Labor's occupation database, describes the core tasks of data entry keyers as:

  • “Compile, sort, and verify the accuracy of data before it is entered.”
  • “Compare data with source documents” and re-enter data where needed.
  • “Locate and correct data entry errors.”

Job titles to search for. O*NET lists titles including Data Entry Clerk, Data Entry Specialist, Data Entry Operator, Data Transcriber, Data Capture Specialist and Records Clerk. Searching several of these finds more listings than “data entry” alone.

Education. O*NET places the occupation in Job Zones 1–2: “Usually requires a high school diploma or GED, though some occupations may not.”

Software. Tools O*NET lists for the role include Intuit QuickBooks, Microsoft Access, Salesforce CRM and Google Drive. Familiarity with spreadsheets and these tools is worth mentioning on applications if you have it.

The physical side. On the same O*NET profile, 93% of respondents said they spend time sitting “continually or almost continually,” so a workable desk and chair matter.

What it pays and how many openings there are

O*NET's figures for data entry keyers, based on Bureau of Labor Statistics 2025 wage data and 2025–2035 projections:

  • Median wage (2025): $19.88 an hour, $41,340 a year.
  • Employment (2025): 131,800 employees.
  • Projected growth (2025–2035): “Decline (-1% or lower).”
  • Projected job openings (2025–2035): 7,700. O*NET notes these openings come from growth and replacement, which means people leaving existing jobs.

On the wider picture, the BLS said in its 2024–34 employment projections release that “The use of automated systems, including AI, is expected to contribute to declining employment of office and administrative support workers.” That statement covers the broad office and administrative support group, not data entry alone.

Method note: who these numbers cover. BLS occupational wage estimates cover employees. Its data entry keyers wage page states: “Estimates do not include self-employed workers.” Freelance and per-task pay is set job by job and is not reflected in the median. Treat $19.88 as a reference point for employee roles, not as what a beginner, freelancer or task worker will earn.

Employer jobs on job boards and government sites

The FTC's job-scam guide points job seekers to USAJobs.gov, CareerOneStop (sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor) and local government job sites, which you can find through USA.gov. Applying costs nothing: “Honest employers, including the federal government, will never ask you to pay to get a job.” Company career pages are another direct route. Reach them by typing the company's address yourself rather than following a link in an unexpected message.

Staffing and placement agencies

Agencies can be a legitimate route into remote clerical work. The FTC explains how honest ones are paid: “the hiring company pays them a fee to find qualified candidates. If a placement firm asks you for a fee — especially one you have to pay in advance — walk away.”

Freelance marketplaces (Upwork example)

On Upwork, clients post data entry projects and freelancers send proposals. Two costs apply:

  • Service fee. According to Upwork's help page, “The fee ranges from 0% to 15% per contract.” The fee is shown when you submit a proposal or receive an offer, it is locked once the proposal, offer or contract is sent, and it can differ from one contract to the next. Upwork's own example: to take home $20 an hour with a 10% fee, you would charge $22.22. Because Upwork's fee can vary per contract, check the figure shown on each one before you set your rate.
  • Connects. Proposals use Connects, and Upwork says “Connects cost $0.15 (USD) each and are sold in bundles.” The number needed for a proposal varies per job, can change while the job is posted, and is shown in the job post. Buying Connects requires a verified billing method.

Upwork's scam guidance warns that “Scammers often lure freelancers with unrealistic pay, vague job descriptions, off-platform communication, or free work requests.” Pressure to talk outside Upwork before a contract starts and payment outside Upwork both break its Terms of Service, and requests for free work break its policies. Upwork's payment protection only applies when payments stay on the platform: funded milestones for fixed-price work, and time tracked in the Upwork desktop app for hourly work.

Microtask platforms (Clickworker example)

Microtask platforms split work into small jobs. Clickworker describes its workers as registered freelancers doing microtasks through an app or desktop, who “are paid per task directly by us.”

According to Clickworker's support page, you need four things to get paid:

  1. Complete payment details.
  2. The payout minimum reached by the start of the bill run: “The minimum is €/$20 for Payoneer and Direct Bank Transfers (non-EU countries), and €/$10 for PayPal, SEPA, and ACH.”
  3. Tax details. US workers give an SSN and upload a signed W-9; EU residents give a tax identification number.
  4. No payment hold on the account.

If a condition isn't met, your earnings stay in your Clickworker balance rather than being lost. Changing your account details puts payments on hold until you confirm the change by email, so avoid editing them just before a payout. PayPal accounts must be verified and able to receive euros.

Amazon Mechanical Turk has closed

Older lists of data entry sites often include Mechanical Turk. It is no longer an option: its homepage now reads, “Amazon Mechanical Turk permanently closed on September 30, 2026.” As Quartz reported via Yahoo Finance, it launched in 2005 as a marketplace for small digital jobs including data labeling, transcription and surveys, and Amazon decided to close it “following an internal assessment of its programs and services.” Be wary of any message offering Mechanical Turk work now.

How data entry scams work

These are the patterns the FTC and FBI describe most often for remote job seekers:

  • Pay-to-work schemes. In work-from-home scams, the FTC says, “instead of making money, you end up paying for starter kits, so-called training, or certifications that are useless.”
  • Fake checks. You deposit a check from your “employer,” who then asks you to send part of it back or buy gift cards. The check bounces and the bank wants the full amount back. The FTC explains: “By law, banks have to make deposited funds available quickly. Even if you see the funds in your account, that doesn't mean it's a good check. Fake checks can take weeks to be discovered and untangled.”
  • Fake recruiter texts. In an April 2026 alert, the FTC described scammers who “claim to be with legit companies you might know” and offer jobs like “online assessor” or a “remote position.” They “often mention pay (sometimes daily or weekly rates) without any details on the actual job,” then ask you to reply “YES” or “INTERESTED.” The FTC's advice: don't.
  • Task scams. The FTC describes scammers asking you to do “simple, repetitive tasks, such as liking videos or rating product images online,” often called “product boosting” or “optimization tasks.” An app shows earnings, then asks you to deposit your own money, “usually in crypto.” The FBI notes these scammers may “pose as a legitimate business, such as a staffing or recruiting agency.”
  • Reshipping. Offers to receive and forward packages are not data entry or any other job: “Reshipping goods is never a real job,” according to the FTC.

The problem is growing. In the first half of 2024, reported losses to job scams topped $220 million, per the FTC's December 2024 Data Spotlight. The same report estimated about 20,000 task-scam reports in the first half of 2024, compared with about 5,000 in all of 2023, and crypto losses to job scams of about $41 million in the first half of 2024, compared with about $21 million in all of 2023. The FTC adds that because most frauds go unreported, these figures likely reflect only a fraction of the harm.

A 5-minute check before you reply or accept

  1. How did it reach you? If it's a generic or unexpected text, WhatsApp or Telegram message, the FTC says to ignore it: “Real employers will never contact you that way.”
  2. Are you asked to pay? Fees for the job, training, certification or a “deposit” to unlock work are a scam sign. The FTC's rule is “Never pay to get paid or get a job,” and the FBI says “Never send money to an alleged employer.”
  3. Is there a check involved? “No honest potential employer will ever send you a check to deposit and then tell you to send on part of the money, or buy gift cards with it” (FTC).
  4. Is the pay specific but the job vague? Pay mentioned with no real job description is a pattern the FTC flags. The FBI also lists hiring that doesn't require any references, and job descriptions that use the term “optimization” for simple tasks, as red flags.
  5. On a platform, does the client want to move off it? Requests to chat or pay outside the platform, or to do free “test” work, are red flags in Upwork's guidance.
  6. Search the name. Search the company or recruiter's name with words like “scam,” “review” or “complaint.” As the FTC notes, finding no complaints “doesn't guarantee that a company is honest.”
  7. Hold back personal and financial details. The FBI advises: “Do not send financial or personal identifiable information to people making unsolicited job offers.” Compare that with a documented process: Clickworker's support page lists tax details such as a signed W-9 as part of its payment setup, not something requested over chat.
  8. Talk it through. The FTC suggests describing the offer to someone you trust before you act.

Getting started this week

  1. Pick one channel from the table: employer jobs if you want a wage, a marketplace if you want project work, a microtask platform if you want small tasks.
  2. Search with the O*NET titles: Data Entry Clerk, Data Entry Specialist, Data Transcriber, Data Capture Specialist, Records Clerk. Searching several titles helps you find data entry jobs from home that are listed under other names.
  3. Set up only the official accounts you need, by typing the site address yourself.
  4. On marketplaces, read the costs before you apply: the Connects shown in the job post and the service fee shown when you submit your proposal.
  5. Run every offer through the checklist above, especially ones that arrive unprompted.

Taxes if you're paid as a freelancer or task worker

Employer jobs normally handle tax withholding. Freelance and task work works differently. According to the IRS self-employed tax center, you are generally self-employed if you are in business for yourself, “including in a part-time business or as a gig worker.” Key points from that page:

  • “You have to file an income tax return if your net earnings from self-employment were $400 or more.” Below $400, you may still have to file if you meet another filing requirement.
  • Self-employment tax “is a Social Security and Medicare tax primarily for individuals who work for themselves.”
  • Self-employed people generally file an annual return and pay estimated taxes quarterly. The forms involved are Form 1040-ES (estimated tax), Schedule C and Schedule SE.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check IRS.gov or a tax professional for your situation.

If you already paid a scammer or shared your details

Act quickly. The FTC says to “immediately contact the company you used to send the money, report the fraud, and ask to have the transaction reversed, if possible.” Its fake check guide gives steps by payment method:

  • Gift card: contact the card issuer and the store where you bought it.
  • Wire transfer: call the transfer company, for example MoneyGram at 1-800-666-3947 or Western Union at 1-800-325-6000. The FTC says a reversal is “unlikely to happen, but it's important to ask.”
  • Money order: contact the issuer, and the U.S. Postal Inspection Service at 877-876-2455 if you sent it by mail.
  • Cryptocurrency: contact the company you used to send it.

Then report it:

  • To the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • To your state attorney general.
  • To the U.S. Postal Inspection Service at uspis.gov/report if a fake check was involved.
  • To the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at www.ic3.gov, including any transaction information, as the FBI asks.

Watch for follow-up scams. The FBI warns: “Do not pay for services that claim to be able to recover any lost cryptocurrency funds.”

FAQ

Do real data entry jobs make you pay for training or software?

No. The FTC says honest employers “will never ask you to pay to get a job,” and paying for starter kits, training or certifications is a common work-from-home scam. Freelance marketplaces are different: they may charge platform costs such as Upwork's service fee and Connects, which their help pages document.

Do I need experience?

O*NET places the occupation in Job Zones 1–2, which “Usually requires a high school diploma or GED, though some occupations may not.” Individual employers and clients set their own requirements, so read each listing.

Is Mechanical Turk still an option?

No. The Mechanical Turk homepage says it “permanently closed on September 30, 2026.”

Why does Upwork charge me to apply?

Upwork proposals use Connects, which cost $0.15 each and are sold in bundles. The number needed varies per job and is shown in the job post. That is separate from the service fee, which applies to what you earn on a contract.
