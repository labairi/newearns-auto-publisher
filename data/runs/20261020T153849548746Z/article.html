Paying an Instagram creator is a small media buy, and the rule for any ad spend applies: check what you're buying first. This guide explains how to vet an Instagram influencer in six steps, from reading their public profile to writing disclosure and reporting terms into the agreement. Before money changes hands, you want to confirm four things: fit, a real audience in your market, steady performance and proper disclosure.

What to confirm before any money changes hands

Each step below answers one of these questions:

  • Fit: Do the creator's content, tone and audience match what you sell?
  • Audience location: Are the people who follow and engage with them in the countries or cities where you can actually sell?
  • Steady performance: Do their typical posts reach and engage people, or is the media kit built on one viral hit?
  • Disclosure habits: Have they labelled past sponsorships clearly enough that your campaign won't create a compliance problem?

Steps 1 and 5 use only what's public. Steps 2 and 3 need the creator's cooperation, because they rely on numbers from the creator's own Insights, which you'll have to ask them to share.

Step 1: Check fit on the public profile

Start with what anyone can see. Spend real time on the profile before you send a message.

  • Recent activity. Are they posting regularly now, or did activity drop off months ago? A creator who posted heavily last year and rarely since may not deliver the reach their media kit describes.
  • Content match. Would your product look natural in their feed? A fitness creator who often posts recipes may suit a meal-prep brand; a travel creator probably isn't the right fit for a local plumber.
  • Comment quality. Read the comments on several posts. Questions about products, replies from the creator and conversations between followers suggest an audience that pays attention. Note anything that looks generic or repetitive for Step 4.
  • Past sponsored posts. Find posts that look like brand work. Note which brands, how recently, and especially how the sponsorship was disclosed. You'll compare those disclosures with FTC guidance in Step 5.
  • Competitors. If they promoted a direct competitor recently, decide whether that matters and whether you need an exclusivity clause.

Step 2: Ask for Insights screenshots (and say exactly which ones)

A media kit is a summary the creator chose to show. Insights screenshots let you see the numbers behind it. Vague requests (“can you send your stats?”) tend to produce cropped, flattering screenshots, so be specific. Ask for:

  • Follower demographics by country and by city
  • Engaged-audience demographics by country and by city
  • Reach, views and accounts engaged for each recent post, not just the best one
  • The timeframe visible in every screenshot, with the same timeframe used across all of them

Here is a message you can adapt:

“Thanks for your interest in working with us. Before we confirm, could you send screenshots from your Instagram Insights showing: (1) follower location by country and city, (2) engaged audience location by country and city, and (3) reach, views and accounts engaged for each of your recent feed posts and reels from the same period. Please use the same timeframe for every audience screenshot (for example, the last 30 days) and keep the date range visible. We ask every creator for the same set so we can compare fairly.”

Also ask whether any of the posts were boosted as ads. Meta's definitions of reach and accounts engaged both include activity from ads, so a paid boost can make a post look stronger than its organic performance.

How to read audience location data

Meta's developer documentation for Instagram Insights explains how the demographic numbers are calculated. It's written for the API, so the screens in the Instagram app may use different labels or layouts, but the underlying rules help you interpret what you're sent:

  • Follower vs. engaged audience. Follower demographics describe the people who follow the account. Engaged-audience demographics describe the account's engaged audience, meaning people who interact with its content rather than everyone who follows it. Both can be broken down by country, city, age and gender (Meta for Developers).
  • Only the top 45 rows. Meta states that “demographic metrics only return the top 45 performers,” so a long tail of smaller cities or countries won't appear (Meta for Developers).
  • Totals can be lower than the follower count. Only viewers Meta has demographic data for are counted, and Meta notes that summing the values may result in a value less than the follower count (Meta for Developers). On its own, that isn't a sign of manipulation.
  • Minimums. Follower demographics aren't returned if the account has fewer than 100 followers, and engaged-audience demographics aren't returned if the account has fewer than 100 engagements during the timeframe (Meta for Developers). A small creator with no engaged-audience data may simply be under that threshold; ask for a longer timeframe.
  • Timeframes. In the current API, demographic data covers either the last 7 days (“this week”) or the last 30 days (“this month”); Meta dropped the 14-day, 30-day, 90-day and previous-month options from version 20.0 onward (Meta for Developers). The app may offer other ranges, so check that every screenshot uses the same one and shows it.
  • Delay. “Data used to calculate metrics may be delayed up to 48 hours,” so very recent posts may look incomplete (Meta for Developers).

The local-business case. If you only sell in one city or region (a café, salon, gym or home-services company), country data tells you little. Ask for the city breakdown and look for the specific cities you serve. Then give more weight to the engaged-audience view than the follower view. Followers can include people who followed long ago from elsewhere, while the engaged audience reflects people who actually interact with the creator's content, which is closer to who is likely to notice your campaign. If your city doesn't appear in the list at all, the creator's audience is probably too spread out for a local offer, however large it is.

Views, reach and impressions in older media kits

Meta's API documentation defines the current metrics this way (Meta for Developers):

  • Reach: the number of unique accounts that have seen the content at least once, including in ads. Meta says this metric is estimated.
  • Views: the number of times content was played or displayed, covering reels, posts and stories. It counts times shown, not unique accounts, so it isn't comparable with reach. Meta labels this metric as in development.
  • Accounts engaged: the number of accounts that interacted with the content, including in ads, through actions such as likes, saves, comments, shares or replies. Also estimated.

Older media kits often quote impressions. In Meta's API, impressions were deprecated for v22.0 and for all versions on April 21, 2025, and the documentation now lists the newer views metric instead (Meta for Developers). Don't compare one creator's old impressions figure with another creator's current views figure. Ask everyone for current reach and views instead. Because in-app labels may not match the API names exactly, ask the creator to point out which number is which if their screen looks different.

Step 3: Compare post-level performance, not just totals

Account-level totals hide a lot. One viral reel can lift a whole month's reach. Work at the post level instead:

  1. Put the reach, views and accounts engaged for each recent post into a spreadsheet, one row per post.
  2. Sort by reach and look at the middle of the list. That typical post is a better estimate of what your sponsored post will do than the best one.
  3. Divide each post's reach by the follower count. You're not looking for a particular percentage; you're looking at how much it varies. If most posts sit in a similar range and one is far above it, plan around the range.
  4. Divide accounts engaged by reach for each post. This shows what share of the people who saw a post did something with it, and lets you compare posts like for like.
  5. Check format. If reels perform very differently from carousels or Stories, plan and price the deliverable in the format you're actually buying.
  6. Flag boosted posts and assess them separately, because reach and accounts engaged include ads.

This guide deliberately gives no target engagement rate. The cut-offs in many vendor checklists come without a source, and the result depends on how engagement is counted, the niche, the format and the account size. Comparing a creator with their own recent posts, and comparing several creators using the same metric and timeframe, is more reliable than chasing a universal number.

Step 4: Look for signs of bought followers or engagement

You can't prove from the outside that an account bought followers, and you shouldn't accuse anyone on a hunch. What you can do is notice patterns worth asking about:

  • Generic comments. Many short, interchangeable comments (“Nice!”, strings of emoji) that never refer to what the post is about.
  • Sudden jumps. If a creator's follower count rose sharply over a short period, ask what caused it. A viral post or press coverage is a perfectly good answer.
  • Engagement that doesn't fit the reach. Lots of likes on posts that, in the Insights screenshots, reached relatively few accounts.
  • Country mismatch. When Instagram announced new authenticity measures in 2020, one situation it described was “when we see the majority of someone's followers are in a different country to their location,” and it said the change would apply only to a small number of accounts (About Instagram). Treat a mismatch as a reason to ask questions, not a verdict. A creator may have moved, or may post in a language with a large audience abroad.

There is also a legal dimension. In August 2024 the FTC announced a final rule that “prohibits anyone from selling or buying fake indicators of social media influence, such as followers or views generated by a bot or hijacked account.” The FTC says this is limited to situations where the buyer “knew or should have known that the indicators were fake and misrepresent the buyer's influence or importance for a commercial purpose” (FTC press release). Separately, the FTC's Endorsement Guides FAQ says that when likes come from people who don't exist or who haven't used the product, “both the purchaser and the seller” of the fake likes could face enforcement action (FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ). The rule addresses whoever buys or sells those indicators. If you have questions about your own position when working with a particular creator, ask a lawyer.

Step 5: Check how they've disclosed past sponsorships

A creator's past disclosures show how they'll likely handle yours, and this matters to you directly. The FTC's FAQ says that if law enforcement becomes necessary, its focus “usually will be on advertisers or their ad agencies and public relations firms,” though action against an individual endorser might be appropriate in certain circumstances (FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ).

Compare their past sponsored posts with the FTC's guidance in Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers:

  • Material connection. A material connection includes a financial relationship, such as the brand paying the creator or giving free or discounted products or services. Gifted products count.
  • Placement. The disclosure should be placed with the endorsement itself. The FTC says disclosures are likely to be missed if they appear only on a profile page, at the end of posts or videos, or anywhere that requires clicking “more.”
  • Hashtags. “Don't mix your disclosure into a group of hashtags or links.” An #ad buried in a long hashtag block at the end of a caption is a problem.
  • Stories. For an endorsement in a picture on Instagram Stories, superimpose the disclosure over the picture and give viewers enough time to notice and read it.
  • Video. For a video endorsement, the disclosure should be in the video, not just in the description.
  • Wording. “Thanks to Acme brand for the free product” is often enough if placed where it's hard to miss, as are “advertisement,” “ad” and “sponsored.” Avoid vague terms like “sp,” “spon” or “collab,” and stand-alone terms like “thanks” or “ambassador.”

On gifted posts, the FTC's FAQ says “gifted” by itself, without a brand reference, is likely to be ambiguous, while “Gifted by XYZ” should be sufficient when all the creator received is a free product (FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ).

Instagram offers a paid partnership label for branded content (Meta Newsroom). Use it, but don't treat it as the whole disclosure. Asked whether a platform's built-in disclosure tool is enough, the FTC answers “Not necessarily,” and says the ultimate responsibility for clear disclosure “rests with the influencer and the brand – not the platform” (FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ). Disclosures 101 likewise suggests using a platform's tool in addition to your own good disclosure, not instead of it.

Step 6: Write disclosure and reporting into the agreement

Once a creator passes the checks above, put expectations in writing. A short agreement or detailed confirmation email should cover:

  • Deliverables: format (reel, feed post, Stories), number of pieces, posting window, and whether you may reuse the content.
  • Disclosure wording and placement: for example “Ad” or “Sponsored” at the start of the caption, superimposed text on Stories, a disclosure inside the video itself, and the paid partnership label in addition.
  • Review step: you see the post, including its disclosure, before it goes live.
  • Post-campaign report: Insights screenshots for each deliverable showing reach, views and accounts engaged, with the date range visible and a stated timeframe. Meta says data may be delayed up to 48 hours, so don't schedule the report for the day of posting (Meta for Developers).
  • Payment milestones: for example, part on signing and the rest once the post is live with the agreed disclosure and the report has been delivered.
  • Exclusivity, if needed: how long the creator won't promote direct competitors.

If you run influencer campaigns for clients, this checklist works as an agency process too; the FTC's FAQ names ad agencies alongside advertisers when describing where its enforcement focus usually falls (FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ). Our guide on how to start a digital marketing agency covers setting up that kind of service.

Outside the US? The FTC says that if posting from abroad, US law applies if it's reasonably foreseeable that the post will affect US consumers, and foreign laws might also apply (FTC Disclosures 101). Check your own country's advertising regulator as well.

Where to find creators who are easier to vet

Instagram's creator marketplace is one route. As of February 2024, Meta said brands join through Meta Business Suite and can search for creators, filtering by creator and audience attributes, while creators join from their professional dashboard in the Instagram app. Meta began testing the marketplace in the US in 2022 and said it planned to invite creators and brands in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, Japan, India and Brazil (Meta Newsroom). Availability may have changed since then, so check Meta Business Suite for your country.

Another option is Instagramaps, which includes a marketplace where creators and businesses create profiles, get matched and send collaboration requests. Creators and businesses can connect their own Instagram account to see where their audience engages by country and city, and compare views, reach and engagement per post. As a business, you would still ask a creator for the Step 2 screenshots; this guide doesn't assume any tool shows you another account's Insights. It has free Creator and Business plans; paid plans start at 9 USD per month (check the pricing page for current prices).

FAQ

What engagement rate is good for an Instagram influencer?

There's no universal number worth relying on. Published cut-offs rarely cite a source, and results vary with niche, format, account size and how engagement is calculated. Instead, divide accounts engaged by reach for each of the creator's recent posts, look at the typical result rather than the best, and compare creators only when you've used the same metric and timeframe for all of them.

Is Instagram's paid partnership label enough disclosure?

The FTC says a platform's built-in tool is “Not necessarily” enough and that responsibility rests with the influencer and the brand, not the platform (FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ). Use the label plus a clear disclosure such as “Ad” placed with the endorsement.

Why don't the location percentages add up to the follower count?

Meta only counts viewers it has demographic data for, returns the top 45 rows, and notes that summed values may be less than the follower count (Meta for Developers). Use the data to see where the audience is concentrated, not as a full headcount.

If a creator bought followers, does the FTC rule apply to my business?

The FTC describes the 2024 rule as banning the selling or buying of fake indicators of social media influence where the buyer knew or should have known they were fake and used them to misrepresent their influence for a commercial purpose (FTC press release). It's framed around buyers and sellers of those indicators. For questions about your specific situation, get legal advice, and use Step 4 to avoid paying for an inflated audience in the first place.
