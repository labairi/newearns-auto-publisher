You have a PDF and need an editable Word file. You can convert PDF to Word in the Word app you may already have, through Google Drive and Google Docs, or with Adobe Acrobat. Whether the layout survives depends mostly on what kind of PDF you start with. This guide starts there, then covers each method using the vendors' own documentation.

First, check what kind of PDF you have

Microsoft's support page on opening PDFs in Word says conversion "works best with files that are mostly text" and warns that the result "might not look exactly the way it looked as a PDF." Before you pick a method, decide which of these three groups your file belongs to.

Mostly text (contracts, reports, invoices)

Microsoft says files that are mostly text, such as business, legal or scientific documents, convert best (Microsoft Support). Its Edit a PDF guide adds a warning: PDFs of book chapters, or "something that looks like a manuscript from a copier," won't be formatted well in Word.

Mostly charts, graphics or designed layouts

If the PDF contains mostly charts or other graphics, Microsoft says the whole page might show up as an image, and the text on it can't be edited (Microsoft Support). If your file is a heavily designed layout, such as slides saved as a PDF, plan for this and keep a text-recognition (OCR) method in reserve.

Scanned pages or photos of documents

A scan is a picture of text. To make it editable, the text must first be recognised with optical character recognition (OCR). Google Drive can convert PDF and photo files to text when you open them with Google Docs (Google Drive Help). Adobe says Acrobat runs text recognition automatically on scanned text when you export to Word (Adobe). Adobe's Reader help also tells you to set the Document Language when you export a scanned document or image (Adobe Help).

Here is a quick check that works in most PDF readers. Try to highlight a single line of text with your cursor. If you can select individual words, the PDF has a text layer. If the whole page selects as one block, or nothing selects at all, the file is probably a scan or an image. In that case, go straight to a method that includes OCR.

Method 1: Convert a PDF to Word in Microsoft Word

Microsoft's instructions apply to Word for Microsoft 365, Word 2024, Word 2021, Word 2019 and Word 2016 (Microsoft Support). Menus may look slightly different between versions.

  1. In Word, go to File > Open.
  2. Find the PDF and open it. You might have to select Browse and look for the PDF in a folder.
  3. Word tells you it will make a copy of the PDF and convert its contents into a format Word can display. The original PDF won't be changed. Select OK.
  4. Make your edits. Then save the file as a Word document or a PDF: in the Save menu, choose the document type from the Save as dropdown and select OK.

These steps come from Microsoft's Edit a PDF guide. Two points from Microsoft are worth knowing before you start:

  • Your original is safe. Word converts a copy. Microsoft says "you always have the original PDF file, in case you don't want to keep the version that Word converts" (Microsoft Support).
  • Pages may not line up exactly. The converted document "might not have a perfect page-to-page correspondence with the original." Lines and pages may break in different places (Microsoft Support).

If you are editing a client document and saving it back to PDF to send, you may also want to restrict who can open it. See How to Password-Protect a PDF (and What the Password Doesn't Protect).

Method 2: Use Google Drive and Google Docs (includes OCR)

This route suits scanned PDFs and photos of documents, because Google Docs recognises the text as it converts. It takes three steps: prepare the file, convert it, then download it as a Word file.

Prepare the file

Google lists these tips "for the best results" (Google Drive Help):

  • Format: PDFs (multipage documents) or photo files (.jpeg, .png and .gif).
  • File size: Google recommends 2 MB or smaller.
  • Resolution: text should be at least 10 pixels high.
  • Orientation: pages must be right side up.
  • Language: Google Drive detects the language of the document.
  • Fonts: use common fonts, such as Arial or Times New Roman.
  • Image quality: use sharp images with even lighting and clear contrast.

These are recommendations, not hard limits. A larger or blurrier file may still convert, but the results are likely to be worse.

Convert it

  1. On your computer, go to Google Drive and upload the PDF if it isn't there already.
  2. Right-click the file.
  3. Click Open with Google Docs.

Steps from Google Drive Help.

Download it as a Word file

  1. With the converted document open in Google Docs, click File > Download at the top.
  2. Choose a file type. The menu lists the available formats; pick Microsoft Word (.docx).
  3. The file downloads to your computer.

Steps from Google Docs Editors Help.

What survives and what doesn't

Google says the format "might not transfer" in full. Bold, italics, font size, font type and line breaks are likely to be kept. Lists, tables, columns, footnotes and endnotes are not likely to be detected (Google Drive Help). This route works well for getting the words out of a scan. It is a weaker choice for table-heavy or multi-column documents.

Method 3: Use Adobe Acrobat (online, desktop or Reader)

Adobe offers three ways to do this. Menu names and tool locations may differ between Acrobat versions and platforms, so treat the paths below as a guide.

Acrobat's free online converter

  1. Go to Adobe's online PDF to Word converter.
  2. Click Select a file, or drag and drop the file into the drop zone.
  3. Select the PDF you want to convert. Acrobat converts it to an editable Word document (DOCX).
  4. Download your file. If you use the tool again, you may be asked to sign in to download it.

Adobe says the tool works on Mac, Windows, iOS, Android and Linux. It also says Acrobat "identifies and reconstructs multi-column layouts, embedded tables, headers and footers, and footnotes in most cases," and that its built-in OCR may be used on complex scanned PDFs (Adobe). That is Adobe's own description of its tool, so expect to check the result.

On free use, Adobe's FAQ (last updated June 2, 2025) says that without signing in, most tools let you complete one free transaction and download the result one time. Signed-in users trying premium tools such as Convert PDF to Word get one free transaction every 30 days on a rolling basis. Adobe says you'll be notified online when you reach the free limits (Adobe Help).

Acrobat desktop

Adobe's how-to page gives these steps (Adobe):

  1. Open the PDF in Acrobat.
  2. Click the Export PDF tool. Adobe's page places it in the right pane, but newer layouts may put it somewhere else.
  3. Choose Microsoft Word as your export format, then Word Document.
  4. Click Export. If the PDF contains scanned text, Adobe says Acrobat runs text recognition automatically.
  5. Name the file, choose DOC or DOCX, and click Save.

On format choice, Adobe says Acrobat can export to both .docx and .doc and recommends .docx on modern systems. You can also export to RTF (Rich Text Format) (Adobe).

Acrobat Reader

The free Reader app can export to Word only if you have a paid subscription to the Adobe Acrobat Export PDF service (Adobe Help). With that subscription in place:

  1. Click Sign In in the upper-right corner of the Reader window and sign in with your Adobe ID.
  2. Select All Tools > Export a PDF. The open file is selected for conversion by default.
  3. From the Export PDF To list, choose the Word format.
  4. Select Convert to the chosen format.
  5. In the Save as dialog, choose a location and select Save.

For a scanned document or image, make sure the Document Language matches the text in the file. To change it, click Change and choose the language from the list (Adobe Help).

What usually breaks, and how to fix it

Microsoft publishes a list of document elements that don't convert well. If your PDF includes these, it suggests you "might want to open it directly rather than converting it to a Word document" (Microsoft Support):

  • Tables with cell spacing
  • Page colors and page borders
  • Tracked changes
  • Frames
  • Footnotes that span more than one page
  • Endnotes
  • Audio, video and PDF active elements
  • PDF bookmarks
  • PDF tags
  • PDF comments
  • Font effects such as Glow or Shadow, which appear as graphics in the Word file

Why does this happen? Microsoft explains that PDF is a fixed file format. Most PDFs don't store information about paragraphs, tables or columns. A table, for example, is stored as a set of lines with no link to the content in its cells. Word applies a set of rules to guess which headings, lists and tables best match the original (Microsoft Support). When the guess is wrong, the layout shifts.

Here are fixes for the most common problems:

  • The page came in as an image and the text won't edit. Microsoft says this can happen with PDFs that are mostly charts or graphics (Microsoft Support). Try a route with text recognition instead: Google Drive's Open with Google Docs (Google Drive Help), or Acrobat's Export PDF, which Adobe says runs text recognition on scanned text (Adobe).
  • A footnote ended up in the body text. If Word doesn't recognise a footnote, it treats it as regular text and might not put it at the bottom of the page (Microsoft Support). Cut the text and reinsert it as a footnote by hand.
  • Tables, lists or columns are flattened after Google conversion. This is expected: Google says these are not likely to be detected (Google Drive Help). For table-heavy files, try Word or Acrobat instead.
  • The file is large and Google Drive doesn't convert it well. Google recommends files of 2 MB or smaller (Google Drive Help). Shrink the file first, or split off just the pages you need. Our guide How to Compress a PDF for Email (and What to Do When It's Still Too Big) covers both.
  • Images look blurry in the Word file. Adobe says image resolution is kept at the level stored in the original PDF. If the images look low-resolution, the original PDF may already have contained reduced-resolution images (Adobe). In that case, ask for the original image files.
  • The PDF has comments, bookmarks or tracked changes. These are on Microsoft's list of elements that don't convert well (Microsoft Support). Keep the PDF open alongside the Word file for reference.

Converting client documents? Check where the file goes

If you freelance, the PDF may be a client's contract, financial report or unpublished work. Before you convert it, know where the file is sent.

  • Online converters upload your file. Adobe's converter page says your file "will be securely handled by Adobe servers and deleted unless you sign in to save it" (Adobe). Adobe's FAQ says that if you don't sign in to save or share the file, Adobe deletes it from the server "within a short time period." It also says Acrobat online services use AES-256 encryption and TLS 1.2, and that uploaded files stored in Adobe cloud storage are automatically labelled Private (Adobe Help).
  • Word desktop converts a copy of the file you opened. Microsoft says Word makes a copy and leaves the original PDF unchanged (Microsoft Support).
  • Google Drive needs the file in your Drive. The conversion starts from a file stored in Google Drive (Google Drive Help), so check whether your client agreement allows that.

FAQ

Is there a free way to convert PDF to Word?

There are options that don't require buying a converter. In Google Drive, you can open the file with Google Docs and then use File > Download to save it as a Word file (Google Drive Help, Google Docs Editors Help). If you already have Word, it can open and convert PDFs (Microsoft Support). Adobe's online tool also allows limited free transactions; its FAQ describes one free transaction without signing in, and one every 30 days for signed-in users trying premium tools (Adobe Help).

Can I convert a scanned PDF to Word?

Yes, but you need a method that recognises text. Google Drive converts PDF and photo files to text when you open them with Google Docs, and works best with sharp, right-side-up pages and files of 2 MB or smaller (Google Drive Help). Adobe says Acrobat runs text recognition automatically on scanned text during export (Adobe). If you only have photos of document pages, Private PDF Editor also offers an image to text tool.

Why do line and page breaks differ from the original?

Microsoft says the converted document might not match the original page for page, and lines and pages may break in different places (Microsoft Support). This happens because most PDFs don't store structure such as paragraphs, tables or columns, so Word has to rebuild the layout from rules (Microsoft Support). Expect to tidy up breaks by hand, especially before you send the file to a client.
