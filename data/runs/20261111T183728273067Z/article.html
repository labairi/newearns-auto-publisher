Finding ideas is not the hard part. Most lists of side hustles for stay-at-home moms give you thirty options and an hourly range for each. They rarely ask whether the work fits the time you have: a nap that can end after ten minutes, the hour after bedtime when the house is finally quiet, or a Saturday morning when another adult is home.

This guide sorts the options by that kind of time. For each one, it uses the platform's own pay and fee pages and points out the catches: waitlists, fees you pay even when nothing sells, payment delays and taxes. Everything here works the same way for stay-at-home dads.

One note before you start: none of these come with a set income. What you earn depends on how much work is available, whether you qualify for it and how fast you work. For a realistic sense of what online income looks like, see Can You Really Make $100 a Day Online?

Start with the kind of time you have

Before you pick a hustle, look at your week and decide which of these four kinds of time you have most of. The right work for a 20-minute nap is different from the right work for a two-hour block after bedtime.

Work you can pause mid-task (good for naps)

Transcription and captioning (Rev as an example)

Transcription means typing out what people say in audio or video files. Captioning does the same but syncs the text to the video. You can usually stop between sections, which makes it a reasonable fit for naps. Rev's freelancer page lists pay per audio or video minute, not per hour:

  • Transcriptionist: $0.40–$1.10 per audio/video minute
  • Captioner: $0.54–$1.10 per audio/video minute
  • Legal transcriptionist: $0.55–$2.00+ per audio/video minute
  • Subtitle translator: $1.70–$4.00+ per audio/video minute; Rev says rates vary by language

According to Rev, pay is sent weekly via PayPal, there is no minimum commitment ("You choose when and how much you work"), and you'll need a computer, a reliable internet connection and strong English skills. To apply, you complete a short skills assessment and then submit a sample.

The catch: Rev's page says it currently has "a waitlist due to high application volume" and that the high volume "may extend response times." You can still apply, so do it early. Pay is per audio minute, not per hour you spend working, so your typing speed and the audio quality set your real hourly rate. Rev itself says earnings vary with the role, the complexity of projects and how many minutes you complete.

Paid research studies (Prolific as an example)

Research platforms pay you to take part in academic and commercial studies, often surveys or short tasks. Prolific's participant page describes the process: join the waitlist, accept your invite, then verify your identity and complete a short practice task, which Prolific says takes about 15 minutes.

Prolific says every task is voluntary and you can "exit any time, for any reason." Once you've earned at least $6/£6, you can cash out from your account. Payments go to PayPal and become instant only after your first 4 cashouts, according to Prolific.

The catch: there's a waitlist before you can start. Once you're in, eligibility filters decide which tasks you see, so not every study is open to you. That makes it hard to predict how much work you'll get in a given week. Prolific's pages don't tell you what you'll earn per hour, so don't plan around a figure from a third-party list.

Selling handmade items or digital printables on Etsy

Making listings, writing product descriptions and designing printables can all be done in short bursts. Before you open a shop, read the fees. Etsy's Fees & Payments Policy (last updated Oct 5, 2026) sets out:

  • Set-up fee: you may have to pay a one-time set-up fee. If it applies, Etsy shows and charges it during shop set-up, and it is non-refundable once paid.
  • Listing fee: $0.20 USD for each item you list. Listings on Etsy.com expire after four months (pattern-only listings don't expire).
  • Transaction fee: 6.5% of the price you display plus what you charge for shipping and gift wrapping. If you sell from the US, it doesn't apply to sales tax.
  • Payment processing fees: these vary based on where your bank account is, and are charged on the total sale amount, including tax and shipping.
  • Offsite Ads: if your shop made less than $10,000 USD in sales over the prior 365 days, you pay 15% on orders attributed to Etsy's offsite ads (a buyer clicks the ad and orders within 30 days). Shops under that threshold can opt out.

The catch: according to Etsy, you're charged the listing fee "whether or not the listed item sells", unless you create a private listing. A shop with many slow-moving items pays for each one, and again when listings expire and you renew them. Look up your country's processing fee in Etsy's payment policy before you set prices.

Work that needs a quiet, uninterrupted block (after bedtime)

Website and app testing (UserTesting as an example)

Testing sites pay you to try websites and apps and say what you think. UserTesting's tester page describes three formats:

  • Surveys: you answer questions with no screen, audio or video recording.
  • Recorded tests: you complete tasks while speaking your thoughts aloud, with screen, audio and sometimes video recorded. You take them on mobile or desktop, on your own time.
  • Live conversations: a video call with a UserTesting customer at a set time.

UserTesting says you'll need a microphone for the practice test, and the one built into your phone or computer is good enough. Pay varies by test type, length and customer demand. Payments are usually sent 14 days after you finish a test, and UserTesting does not withhold any taxes, according to its tester page.

The catch: a crying toddler or a sibling argument in the background makes recorded tests and live calls hard. Save those for after bedtime or for a slot when another adult is home. Because surveys aren't recorded, they're the format most likely to survive an interruption during the day, but plan for that rather than count on it.

Freelance writing, virtual assistance or design

If you have a professional skill, freelancing can pay better than task platforms, but it needs focused time for the work itself and often a fixed slot for client calls. You can find clients directly (former employers, local businesses, your own network) or through a freelance marketplace.

Marketplaces charge fees, and those fees change. Check the platform's own fee page before you set your rates so you know what you'll actually take home. For ready-made pitch and proposal templates, see our ChatGPT prompts for freelancers and side hustles.

Work you can do with kids around

Caring for other children in your home

Looking after other families' children alongside your own is one of the few options built for kids-around time. It is also regulated. Childcare.gov says licensing is the main way the US and its territories regulate child care: state and territory governments set minimum health and safety requirements that programs must meet to operate legally, and federal law requires states and territories to set and enforce those requirements.

The rules differ from state to state. Before you take a paying child, look up your own state's licensing rules and find out whether and when they apply to you. Childcare.gov also notes that licensing doesn't guarantee quality, so parents may ask about more than your license.

Pet sitting and dog walking

Pet sitting and dog walking are local, in-person jobs. Some fit around school runs or can be done with children along, depending on the pet and the owner. If you use a pet-sitting app, read its fee page before you set your rate, since the app's cut affects what you keep.

Spotting a "mom boss" pitch that's really a pyramid scheme

Parents at home are a common target for "be your own boss" recruiting pitches. Some are legitimate direct-sales businesses; some are pyramid schemes dressed up to look like one. The SEC's Investor.gov warns that promoters "may go to great lengths to make the program look like a business, such as a legitimate multi-level marketing (MLM) program," and lists these hallmarks:

  • Emphasis on recruiting. Be skeptical if you'd earn more for recruiting others than for selling products.
  • No genuine product or service is sold.
  • Promises of high returns in a short time period.
  • Easy money or passive income.
  • No demonstrated revenue from retail sales.
  • Complex commission structure.

As a general rule, Investor.gov says, legitimate MLM companies get their revenue mainly from selling products, not from recruiting members. It also warns: "All pyramid schemes eventually collapse, and most investors lose their money." Ask any company that recruits you where its revenue comes from, and walk away if the answer is mostly new members.

Taxes on side income, in plain English

This is general US information, not tax advice. If your situation is complicated, talk to a tax professional.

  • Self-employment tax: according to the IRS, you must pay self-employment tax and file Schedule SE (Form 1040) if your net earnings from self-employment were $400 or more. The rate is 15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. You can deduct the employer-equivalent portion when you work out your adjusted gross income, and you may have to pay estimated taxes quarterly.
  • Form 1099-K: the IRS says a payment app or online marketplace must send you a 1099-K if payments for goods or services total over $20,000 in more than 200 transactions, but it may send one for lower amounts. If clients pay you directly by card, you'll get one from your card processor no matter how many payments you received. Either way, you must report your income whether or not you receive the form.
  • Gifts aren't business income: the same IRS page says money from friends and family as a gift or repayment shouldn't be reported on a 1099-K and isn't taxable income.

Platforms may not hold anything back for you. UserTesting, for example, says it does not withhold any taxes. Setting aside part of each payout makes tax time easier. Also note that the work in this guide is hands-on, even when it's sold as "passive." For how the IRS uses that word, see passive income vs active income.

What to do this week

  1. Map your real time. For three days, note when you get gaps, how long they last and whether they're quiet. Match each type of gap to the table at the top.
  2. Join the waitlists on day 1. Rev and Prolific both have waitlists, so apply now even if you won't start for weeks.
  3. Do UserTesting's practice test in a quiet block. You'll need a working microphone; a built-in one is fine.
  4. If you plan to sell on Etsy, list a few items to start, not dozens, and budget for the $0.20 listing fee you pay whether or not each item sells.
  5. If you're thinking about childcare, look up your state's licensing rules before you agree to anything with another family.
  6. Start a simple income and expense log. A spreadsheet with date, platform, amount and costs is enough to make tax time easier.

FAQ

Which of these can I start without experience?

Research studies and website testing don't ask for a professional skill on the pages reviewed here. Prolific asks for identity verification and a short practice task, and UserTesting has a practice test. Transcription with Rev is different: you have to pass a skills assessment and submit a sample.

Do I have to pay tax on a small side hustle?

You must report all your income, whether or not you get a Form 1099-K, according to the IRS. Self-employment tax and Schedule SE apply once your net self-employment earnings reach $400, per the IRS self-employment tax page.

Is a direct-sales company a pyramid scheme?

Not necessarily. Investor.gov says legitimate MLM companies get their revenue mainly from selling products, not from recruiting. Warning signs include more pay for recruiting than for sales, promises of high returns fast, and no revenue shown from retail sales.

Do I need a license to babysit in my home?

It depends on your state. Childcare.gov explains that states and territories set and enforce child care licensing requirements, so check your own state's rules before you take paying children.
