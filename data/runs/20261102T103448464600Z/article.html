YouTube does not publish a per-view rate, so any single figure you see for how much does YouTube pay per view is an estimate, not a price list. What YouTube does publish is how revenue is split. Members of the YouTube Partner Program (YPP) receive 55% of net revenue from ads shown on their public videos on the Watch Page, and 45% of the revenue allocated to them from the Shorts Creator Pool.

YouTube also makes no promise of income. Its partner earnings page states: "There are no guarantees under the YouTube partner agreement about how much or whether you'll be paid."

Your own number is your RPM in YouTube Analytics. This guide explains how that number is built, who can earn at all, what changes on Feb 1, 2027, and how much of your earnings actually reaches your bank account. Third-party estimates appear in one clearly labelled section.

The short answer: there is no fixed rate per view

YouTube pays partners a share of revenue from ads and other sources such as memberships, Super Chat and YouTube Premium. It does not pay a set amount per view. A view where no ad plays earns nothing from ads.

This is why YouTube's own metric for creators, RPM (revenue per mille), is the closest thing to a real "pay per view" figure. According to YouTube's ad revenue analytics help page, RPM is your total revenue after YouTube's share, per 1,000 views (engaged views for Shorts). It counts every view, including views where no ad was shown, so your RPM can fall when unmonetized views rise, even if your revenue stays the same.

Who gets paid at all: the YouTube Partner Program thresholds

Ad revenue sharing requires YPP membership. YouTube's eligibility page notes: "If you do not currently meet the higher eligibility criteria of YPP you may still see ads being served on your content." In that case, you are not yet eligible for a share of that revenue.

The thresholds are changing. In an announcement dated Aug 10, 2026, YouTube said new terms take effect on Feb 1, 2027. Creators already in YPP are not affected by the higher entry thresholds. The table compares the current rules from the YPP eligibility page with the announced changes.

A few points apply whichever thresholds you are working toward, according to the same eligibility page:

  • Hitting the numbers is not automatic entry. Every channel is reviewed.
  • Other requirements apply. You must follow the monetization policies, live in an eligible country or region, and have no active Community Guidelines strikes. You also need 2-Step Verification turned on, access to advanced features, and one AdSense for YouTube account.
  • Existing partners must accept the updated terms in Studio by Jan 31, 2027 to keep full monetization.
  • Inactivity counts. Monetization may be turned off if a channel goes 6 months or more without uploads or posts.

There is also a lower YPP tier. YouTube's YPP explainer says it unlocks fan funding and promoting your own products, but not ad revenue sharing, which requires the higher thresholds. YouTube's 2027 announcement says the fan funding and shopping thresholds are not changing.

How the money is split

YouTube divides revenue by "module". Each module covers one revenue source and has its own share. All shares below come from YouTube's partner earnings overview, Shorts monetization policies and the Aug 2026 announcement.

Revenue sourceCreator's share
Ads on long-form videos (Watch Page, including YouTube's player embedded on other sites and apps)55% of net revenue
Shorts Feed ads45% of the revenue allocated to you from the Creator Pool
Fan funding: memberships, Super Chat, Super Stickers, Super Thanks70% of net revenue
Shorts ads targeted at 5 or fewer channels (from Feb 1, 2027)45% of net revenue, paid directly
YouTube Premium and Premium Lite (new terms from Feb 1, 2027)30% of net Premium revenue and 60% of net Premium Lite revenue go into a pool. Creators then get 55% (long-form) or 45% (Shorts) of their share

Transaction taxes such as sales tax, VAT and GST are not counted as revenue and are left out of the revenue-share calculation. CPM vs. RPM: the two numbers in YouTube Studio

What each one counts

YouTube defines CPM this way: "CPM is the cost per 1000 ad impressions before YouTube revenue share." It is what advertisers pay, not what you receive. RPM is what you receive: total revenue after YouTube's share, per 1,000 views (engaged views for Shorts).

RPM includes revenue from ads, memberships, YouTube Premium, Super Chat and Super Stickers. According to the same help page, it excludes merch sales, brand deals (except YouTube Creator Partnerships) and indirect income such as consulting.

YouTube's own worked example

YouTube's ad revenue analytics page walks through this example:

  • A video gets 5,000 views.
  • 1,000 of those views show one ad and 500 show two ads. That makes 1,500 monetized playbacks and 2,000 ad impressions.
  • Advertisers pay $7 in total.
  • CPM = $7 ÷ 2,000 impressions × 1,000 = $3.50.
  • Playback-based CPM = $7 ÷ 1,500 playbacks × 1,000 = $4.67.

Notice that 3,500 of the 5,000 views showed no ad at all. Those views still count in RPM.

Why your RPM is always lower than your CPM

Two things pull RPM below CPM, according to YouTube's help page. First, RPM is calculated after YouTube takes its share. Second, RPM includes views that earned nothing. So if you see a CPM in Studio, do not multiply it by your views to estimate pay. Use RPM.

Why your pay per view varies

YouTube names these factors in its ad revenue analytics and mid-roll help pages:

  • Time of year. Advertisers bid more just before holidays, which raises CPM (source).
  • Viewer geography. CPM differs depending on where your viewers are (source).
  • Ad formats. A higher share of non-skippable ads can mean a higher CPM (source).
  • Unmonetized views. More views without ads lower your RPM (source).
  • Video length and mid-rolls. YouTube says: "On monetized videos that are 8 minutes or longer, you can turn on ads during the middle of the video". An ad slot does not mean an ad will run. Slots at natural breaks are more likely to get an ad than slots placed mid-sentence.
  • Advertiser-friendly guidelines. For Shorts, views on content that falls outside these guidelines don't count toward revenue (source).
  • Premium and fan revenue. Because RPM includes Premium, memberships, Super Chat and Super Stickers, channels with more of these can have a higher RPM from the same ad income (source).

How Shorts pay: the Creator Pool in four steps

Shorts are not paid per Short. YouTube's Shorts monetization policies describe a shared pool:

  1. Pool the ad revenue. Ad revenue from the Shorts Feed is added together each month.
  2. Split off music costs. If a Short uses no music, all of its revenue goes to the Creator Pool. With one music track, half goes to the pool; with two tracks, a third does.
  3. Allocate by share of views. The pool is divided among monetizing creators by their share of engaged views, country by country. YouTube's example: if you have 5% of the eligible views in a country, you are allocated 5% of that country's pool.
  4. Apply the 45% share. YouTube's wording: "Monetizing creators will keep 45% of their allocated revenue, regardless if music was used or not."

Premium and Premium Lite revenue from Shorts works in a similar way. According to the same policy page, creators get 45% of the net revenue allocated to Shorts creators, based on their share of subscriber Shorts views per country.

What changes on Feb 1, 2027:

  • New minimum for Shorts revenue. Channels will need 10M qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days to share in Shorts ad and subscription revenue. Channels below that stay in YPP, keep earning on long-form videos, and Shorts revenue sharing resumes automatically once they are back above 10M.
  • How the 90 days are measured. The window is the 90 days ending on the 15th of the month before the month being paid.
  • Targeted Shorts ads. Shorts ads aimed at 5 or fewer channels will pay creators 45% of net revenue directly, instead of through the pool.

What published estimates say (and how to read them)

The figures in this section are third-party estimates, not YouTube rates. All of them come from a Hootsuite article published Oct 13, 2026.

  • Advertisers pay about $5–$15 per 1,000 ad views. Hootsuite labels this "an industry estimate rather than a published YouTube rate".
  • Creators report long-form RPM of roughly $2–$10 per 1,000 views. Hootsuite describes this as a creator-reported estimate.
  • Creators report Shorts RPM of roughly $0.01–$0.06 per 1,000 Shorts views, also an estimate.
  • CPM tends to peak in Q4 and drop in January and February, according to Hootsuite.

To turn any RPM into an earnings estimate, use this formula: views ÷ 1,000 × your RPM. If you plug in Hootsuite's creator-reported long-form range of $2–$10, 1 million long-form views works out to roughly $2,000–$10,000, or about $0.002–$0.01 per view. Treat that as a wide estimate, not a forecast. Your own RPM, explained below, is the only figure that applies to your channel. From earnings to your bank account

The revenue in Analytics is not the amount you are paid, and it does not arrive straight away. YouTube's partner earnings overview describes the monthly cycle:

  1. Estimates are adjusted. Estimated revenue is adjusted for invalid traffic, Content ID claims and disputes, and some campaign types. This happens about a week after the revenue is generated, and again mid-way through the following month.
  2. Earnings are finalized. Finalized earnings are added to your AdSense for YouTube account between the 7th and 12th of the month.
  3. You are paid between the 21st and 26th, if you have reached your payment threshold, have no holds on your account, have provided U.S. tax info, are not suspended or paused from monetization, and comply with YouTube's policies.

Payment threshold. YouTube's AdSense page uses this example: "If the YouTube payment threshold is $100, and you reached that threshold amount in June, then we will issue you a payment by July 26". YouTube and AdSense payments accounts have separate thresholds.

The $20 threshold test. According to Tubefilter (Oct 28, 2026), YouTube is testing a $20 payout threshold instead of the previous $100 minimum. The test runs in the U.S., India and Indonesia, "starting from October 2026 payments for a randomly selected group of creators". Tubefilter reports that creators in the test can change their threshold in AdSense. It also reports that a wider rollout would not change YPP eligibility. This is a test, not a change for everyone.

Verification before payment. Before you can be paid, YouTube's AdSense for YouTube page lists these steps:

  • Enter the PIN that is mailed to you once you pass the address verification threshold. Some locations also require identity verification.
  • Provide your tax info.
  • Add a payment method once you pass the payment-method selection threshold.

U.S. tax withholding. According to YouTube's U.S. tax requirements page:

  • U.S. creators with valid tax info have nothing withheld.
  • Non-U.S. creators who submit tax info have 0–30% withheld from earnings from U.S. viewers, depending on tax treaties. YouTube's own example is India, where a 15% treaty rate applies.
  • Without tax info, business accounts outside the U.S. have 30% of U.S. earnings withheld. U.S. businesses and individual accounts face the rule YouTube states as: "If tax info isn't provided by Dec. 10th, Google may be required to deduct up to 24% of your total earnings worldwide."
  • Tax forms expire, and you will be asked to resubmit roughly every three years.
  • Withholding is not shown in YouTube Analytics. Once you provide valid tax info, rates are adjusted in the next payment cycle.

Multi-channel networks. If your channel is in an MCN, YouTube pays the MCN, and the MCN pays you. Your agreement with the MCN then decides what reaches you.

Catches that most guides skip

  • Analytics revenue is an estimate. It can go down after adjustments for invalid traffic and Content ID.
  • Some Shorts views never count. Views from before you accepted the Shorts Monetization Module aren't eligible.
  • Unoriginal or artificial views don't count. This covers unedited clips, reuploads and compilations with nothing original added. Bot views and content outside the advertiser-friendly guidelines are also excluded from the Shorts pool calculation.
  • Long Shorts with claimed content earn nothing. Shorts over 1 minute that contain claimed content are blocked from monetization.
  • Some Shorts revenue never reaches the pool. This includes revenue from Shorts by non-monetizing creators and music partners, the Shorts Masthead, navigational pages and Image Posts (source).
  • Mid-roll slots are not ad sales. An inserted slot may not serve an ad.
  • Withholding can quietly reduce your payout. It isn't visible in Analytics.
  • Inactivity can switch off monetization after 6 months or more without uploads or posts.
  • Missing the terms deadline can cost you. Accept the updated YPP terms by Jan 31, 2027 to keep full monetization.

How to check your own RPM this week

The most useful answer to how much YouTube pays per view on your channel is in YouTube Studio. These steps follow YouTube's ad revenue analytics help page. Menus may differ slightly depending on your device and Studio version.

  1. Sign in to YouTube Studio and select Analytics in the left menu.
  2. Open the Revenue tab.
  3. Find RPM. This is what you earned per 1,000 views after YouTube's share. Note CPM next to it, but don't use CPM to estimate your pay.
  4. Set the date range to the last 28 or 90 days, then compare the same range a year earlier to see seasonal swings.
  5. Look at your RPM for individual videos to compare long-form and Shorts, and longer videos against shorter ones.
  6. Multiply your next video's expected views ÷ 1,000 by your recent RPM. This gives a rough estimate, before any adjustments or withholding.

To raise RPM, YouTube's help page suggests monetizing all eligible videos, turning on mid-roll ads (on videos of 8 minutes or more), and turning on memberships and Super Chat.

FAQ

How much does YouTube pay for 1 million views?

YouTube publishes no figure for this. It depends on your RPM: 1,000,000 ÷ 1,000 × RPM. Using Hootsuite's creator-reported long-form estimate of $2–$10 RPM, that is roughly $2,000–$10,000 before tax withholding. This is an estimate, not a YouTube rate.

Do Shorts pay less than long-form videos?

Shorts earn through a pooled system at a 45% share of allocated revenue, compared with 55% of net ad revenue for long-form. Hootsuite's creator-reported estimate for Shorts RPM is $0.01–$0.06 per 1,000 views, which is far below its long-form range. These are estimates, not YouTube rates.

Can I earn from YouTube if I'm not in the Partner Program?

Not from ads. YouTube says it may still show ads on your content before you qualify, but you won't get a share of that revenue. The lower YPP tier gives access to fan funding and promoting your own products, not ad revenue.

When does YouTube money arrive?

Finalized earnings are added to AdSense for YouTube between the 7th and 12th of the following month. They are paid by the 21st–26th once you have reached your threshold and met the payment conditions. In YouTube's example, a $100 threshold reached in June is paid by July 26.
