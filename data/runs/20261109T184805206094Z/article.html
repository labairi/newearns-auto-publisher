The simplest way to frame passive income vs active income is this. Active income is paid for work as you do it. Passive income keeps arriving after the setup work is done. In practice, almost nothing that earns money is completely hands-off. Books need marketing, shops need listings renewed, and rentals need tenants and repairs.

There is also a second meaning of "passive" that matters at tax time. In the US, the IRS uses "passive activity" in a much narrower sense than everyday speech does. Some things people casually call passive income, such as dividends or royalties from a book you wrote yourself, fall outside the IRS definition. This guide covers both meanings, with examples side by side.

The tax notes here are general US information, not tax advice. If you live outside the US, check your own country's rules. For your own situation, a tax professional can confirm how your income is classified.

The quick difference

  • Active income is money paid in exchange for work you are doing now. Examples include a salary or hourly wages, freelance fees and sales commissions. If you stop working, the pay stops.
  • Passive income, in the everyday sense, is money from something you built or bought earlier. Examples include a self-published book, a digital download, dividend-paying investments or a rental property. Each payment doesn't depend on you putting in another hour that day.

"Passive" describes how the money arrives, not how much work it took. An ebook or a set of design templates can take months to create. That work is simply done up front instead of being billed by the hour, and most of these assets still need some upkeep afterward.

What "passive" means at tax time (US)

The IRS recognizes only two kinds of passive activity

According to IRS Publication 925, "There are two kinds of passive activities":

  • "Trade or business activities in which you don't materially participate during the year."
  • "Rental activities, even if you do materially participate in them, unless you're a real estate professional."

That is a much narrower idea than the everyday "money while you sleep." Under these rules, a business is only passive for you if you don't materially participate in it, however automated it may be.

Material participation: the hour tests

Pub 925 says a trade or business activity isn't a passive activity if you materially participated in it. You materially participated for a tax year if you meet any one of several tests. They include these (IRS Publication 925):

  • You participated in the activity for more than 500 hours.
  • Your participation was substantially all the participation in the activity by all individuals for the year, including people who didn't own an interest in it.
  • You participated for more than 100 hours during the year, and at least as much as any other individual, including non-owners.
  • A significant-participation test: more than 500 hours combined across activities in which you participated for more than 100 hours each.
  • You materially participated in the activity for any 5 of the 10 immediately preceding tax years, whether or not those years were consecutive.
  • Based on all the facts and circumstances, you participated on a regular, continuous and substantial basis during the year. You can't meet this test if you participated for 100 hours or less.

There are other tests too, so read Pub 925 for the full list. It also says: "In general, any work you do in connection with an activity in which you own an interest is treated as participation in the activity." For a solo online business, where you do nearly all the work yourself, that matters a lot.

Portfolio income is a separate bucket

Pub 925 says passive activity income doesn't include portfolio income. That covers "interest, dividends, annuities, and royalties not derived in the ordinary course of a trade or business," plus gain or loss from selling property that produces that kind of income or is held for investment (IRS Publication 925). So the dividends people often hold up as the classic example of passive income are, in IRS terms, portfolio income.

Royalties can land in different places depending on your situation. Schedule E is used to report income from rental real estate and royalties, among other things. However, the instructions say self-employed writers, inventors and artists report royalty income on Schedule C instead. They also note that if you received $10 or more in royalties during 2025, the payer should send you a Form 1099-MISC or similar statement by January 31, 2026 (IRS 2025 Instructions for Schedule E).

Why the label matters

  • Losses. Pub 925 describes the passive activity rules as rules that "may limit the amount of your deductible loss" from a trade, business, rental or other income-producing activity (IRS Publication 925). The label mainly matters for how losses can be deducted.
  • Net investment income tax. A 3.8% net investment income tax (NIIT) applies to individuals, estates and trusts with net investment income above set thresholds. For individuals, those thresholds are based on modified adjusted gross income: $200,000 for single or head of household, $250,000 for married filing jointly or qualifying surviving spouse, and $125,000 for married filing separately. Net investment income includes interest, dividends, certain annuities, royalties and rents (with exceptions), and income from a trade or business that is a passive activity. The IRS says the NIIT doesn't apply to wages, unemployment compensation or income from an active business (IRS Topic 559).
  • Self-employment tax. Active self-employment income works differently. You usually must pay self-employment tax if net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more. Generally, 92.35% of those net earnings is subject to the tax. The rate is made up of 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. There is an annual cap on earnings subject to the Social Security part, and you can deduct one-half of the self-employment tax (IRS Topic 554).

For most online earners, the practical point in the passive income vs active income question is this: calling income "passive" in conversation doesn't change how the IRS treats it.

Why most online "passive income" is really a business

Ebooks, courses, templates, printables and designs are the usual examples of online passive income. In IRS terms, they rarely fit the narrow definition.

  • You made it, so it isn't passive activity income. Pub 925 excludes from passive activity income "any income from intangible property, such as a patent, copyright, or literary, musical, or artistic composition, if your personal efforts significantly contributed to the creation of the property" (IRS Publication 925).
  • Self-employed creators use Schedule C. The Schedule E instructions tell self-employed writers, inventors and artists to report royalty income and expenses on Schedule C, not Schedule E (IRS Schedule E instructions).
  • Self-employment tax usually follows. If you are a sole proprietor or otherwise in business for yourself, you usually owe self-employment tax once net earnings reach $400 (IRS Topic 554).

Platforms also keep charging for as long as your product stays live. On Etsy, listings expire after four months. If automatic renewals are on, you are charged the $0.20 listing fee for each renewed listing until you cancel them (Etsy Fees & Payments Policy). On Amazon KDP, the 70% royalty option is calculated after delivery costs, which average $0.06 per unit and vary by file size. It applies only to eligible books sold to customers in the 70% territories, and sales outside those territories earn 35% (KDP eBook Royalties). KDP also says works in the public domain, or consisting mainly of public domain content, are only eligible for the 35% option.

Then there is the upkeep that rarely appears in a passive income pitch:

  • Answering customer questions and handling refund requests
  • Updating products when tools, platforms or information change
  • Renewing listings and adjusting prices
  • Ongoing marketing, because new buyers have to find the product somehow

What it costs to set up, and what to check before you start

No reliable figure exists for how long a digital product or creator channel takes to earn money, or how much it will earn. Results vary too widely. You can, however, check the known costs and conditions before you commit:

  • Upfront fees. Etsy says you may have to pay a one-time set-up fee to open a shop, shown during set-up. It charges $0.20 per listing whether or not the item sells, unless the listing is private. Etsy also states that all service fees, including prepaid fees, are non-refundable (Etsy Fees & Payments Policy).
  • The platform's cut. Etsy charges a 6.5% transaction fee on the displayed price plus shipping and gift wrapping, with payment processing fees charged separately (Etsy). KDP pays 35% of the list price without VAT under its 35% option, or the 70% option subject to its conditions (KDP).
  • Your build time. Count the hours it will take to create the asset, and treat them as real work, because they are.
  • What happens if you stop. Find out whether income continues if you stop renewing, updating or promoting the product.

Is it passive? Five questions to ask

  1. If I stopped working on this for three months, would the income continue, shrink or stop?
  2. Does the platform charge recurring fees just to keep my product visible?
  3. Who answers customers, and how often will that be me?
  4. Did I create this myself, which may make it business income rather than passive activity income for tax purposes?
  5. Am I paying someone else a large sum up front to make it "passive" for me?

These questions are a framework for thinking, not tax tests. The IRS tests are the material participation rules described above.

Red flags in "passive income" pitches

The phrase "passive income" shows up in some deceptive business-opportunity marketing, and the Federal Trade Commission has acted against companies using it.

Click Profit. In March 2025, the FTC announced action against Click Profit, in a press release titled as an online business opportunity that "has cost consumers at least $14 million." The FTC alleged that Click Profit and its owners promised consumers they could make large sums in "passive income" using a proprietary system powered by artificial intelligence. The complaint alleges that buyers were charged a "management fee" of at least $45,000, plus thousands of dollars more, supposedly for store inventory. It also alleges that most consumers lose their entire payments, and that Amazon blocked, suspended or terminated about 95% of Click Profit's stores. The FTC notes that it files a complaint when it has "reason to believe" the law is being or is about to be violated, and that the case will be decided by the court (FTC press release on Click Profit).

Publishing.com. Publishing.com LLC and its two principals agreed to pay $1.5 million, and to substantiate future earnings claims, to settle FTC charges. According to the FTC, the company's self-publishing course could cost up to $1,995 and was marketed as a "foolproof, passive income system." The FTC's complaint says most buyers never achieved the income promised in the advertising. The settlement is a proposed consent order, open to public comment before the Commission decides whether to make it final (FTC press release on Publishing.com).

Warning signs worth taking seriously:

  • Promises of certain or effortless income
  • Large upfront fees, especially "management" or "coaching" fees in the thousands
  • Success stories and screenshots you can't verify
  • "Done-for-you" stores or systems that claim to need no work from you
  • Pressure to buy quickly, or to pay with credit you can't afford to lose

Building a mix of active and passive income

For most people starting out, the answer isn't to choose one over the other. Active income, such as a job or freelance work, pays the bills while you slowly build an asset on the side. That asset might be a book, a template shop or a content channel. As it grows, it may cover more of your income, though nothing guarantees that it will.

A few practical points:

  • Keep your active income steady while you build. Assets usually take time, and how long is unpredictable.
  • Track the hours you spend on the "passive" side. They tell you whether it's really passive, and the IRS participation tests count hours too.
  • Set aside money for taxes on both streams. Freelance and self-published income are usually self-employment income.

FAQ

Is freelancing passive income?

No. Freelancing is active income: you are paid for work as you do it. The IRS treats self-employment income from a business you materially participate in as personal service income, not passive activity income (Pub 925). You usually owe self-employment tax if your net earnings are $400 or more (IRS Topic 554).

Are dividends passive income?

In everyday terms, yes. For tax purposes, the IRS classifies interest and dividends as portfolio income, which is not passive activity income (Pub 925).

Is rental income passive?

Generally, yes. Under IRS rules, rental activities are passive even if you materially participate in them. If you qualify as a real estate professional, rental real estate activities in which you materially participate aren't passive (Pub 925).

Is ebook income passive?

It may feel passive once the book is out, but the tax treatment usually says otherwise. If you are in business as a self-employed writer, you report royalties on Schedule C (Schedule E instructions). Income from a work you substantially created yourself is excluded from passive activity income (Pub 925).
