Your PDF won't attach, or the email comes back undelivered. This guide shows how to compress a PDF for email with tools you probably already have, explains which attachment limit applies to your account, and covers what to do when the file is still too big after compressing.

Check which attachment limit applies to you

Before you shrink anything, find your target. Limits depend on your email provider, the type of account, and sometimes the app you send from. The figures below come from each provider's own help pages.

If you send from a work or school account, the number that matters is whatever your administrator has configured. If you're not sure, ask your IT contact before you start compressing.

Why a file just under the limit can still fail

Email attachments are encoded for transport, and that makes the message bigger than the file on your disk. Microsoft's Exchange Online documentation says messages routed outside Microsoft's datacenters are subject to an additional 33% encoding increase, and that Outlook on the web restricts the message size you can send to 25% less than the configured setting to account for it. Microsoft's example: with a 100 MB limit configured, you can send messages no larger than 75 MB (Exchange Online limits).

The recipient's side matters too. Apple notes that the recipient's email app might have a smaller message size limit that prevents the attachment from being delivered (Mail Drop limits), and Microsoft says some email clients may have lower message size limits or cap individual attachments below the Exchange Online limit (Exchange Online limits).

The practical takeaway: leave headroom. There's no official "safe" size, but a file that sits right at the edge of your limit is the one most likely to bounce. If your compressed PDF is still close to the limit, compress further, trim pages, or send a link instead.

Compress a PDF on a Mac with Preview

Preview comes with macOS, so there's nothing to install. Apple's steps (Apple Support: Reduce the size of a PDF in Preview):

  1. Open the PDF in Preview.
  2. Choose File > Export.
  3. In the Quartz Filter pop-up menu, choose Reduce File Size.
  4. In the Export As field, give the file a new name, such as proposal-email.pdf, so the original stays untouched.
  5. Click Save.

If you keep the same name, Preview will offer to replace the original. Apple's guidance for keeping the original is to click Cancel, enter a new filename in the Export As field, then click Save.

Apple's current guide also lists extra options in the export dialog:

  • Create Linearized PDF: optimizes the PDF for fast loading and viewing in web browsers.
  • Optimize images for screen: optimizes images for screen viewing to reduce file size.
  • Save images as JPEG: converts images to JPEG format, which uses image compression.

These options may not appear, or may be labelled differently, on older versions of macOS. Apple also warns that a compressed PDF may be of lower quality than the original, and that the resulting size and image quality vary depending on the options you select. Always open the exported copy before sending it (see the checklist further down).

Compress a PDF in Adobe Acrobat

Adobe's learning guide says compressing a PDF makes it easier to share via email or upload to websites with file size limits (Adobe Experience League: Compress & Optimize a PDF). The steps below follow that guide, last updated April 23, 2026. Adobe updates Acrobat's interface often, so menu names in your version may differ.

Acrobat desktop app

  1. Open the file and select All tools from the toolbar.
  2. Choose Compress a PDF.
  3. In the Compress a PDF panel, select Single file or Multiple files.
  4. Select Save. Consider saving under a new name so you keep the original.

Adobe describes the result as reduced "to the smallest size possible while retaining the document's quality." It's still worth checking the output yourself, especially for scanned pages and charts.

Acrobat on the web

  1. Sign in to acrobat.adobe.com in a browser.
  2. Select Convert > Compress a PDF from the top menu.
  3. Choose Select files, pick your file, and choose Open.
  4. Select a compression level and choose Compress.

Acrobat on the web uploads your file to Adobe's service, so the privacy checklist in the browser-based section below applies here too.

Advanced Optimization (Acrobat Pro, Premium or Studio)

According to Adobe, optimizing a PDF is only available in Acrobat Pro, Premium or Studio on the desktop. Select Advanced Optimization from the Compress a PDF panel. From the Make Compatible With dropdown, choose Retain Existing to keep the current PDF version, or choose an Acrobat version. The dialog also lets you adjust settings for elements such as images, fonts and transparency, which is useful when the standard compression doesn't shrink the file enough or softens images too much.

Make a smaller PDF from the original Word or PowerPoint file

If you created the PDF yourself, going back to the source file can be a cleaner route than squeezing the finished PDF, because you control what goes into the export.

PowerPoint: Microsoft's export instructions say that if file size is more important than print quality, select Minimum size (publishing online). The same choice is documented for Access (Microsoft Support: Save or convert to PDF). Other Office apps may show a similar choice depending on your version.

Word: Microsoft's steps for saving a document as PDF are:

  1. If the file was previously saved, select File > Save a Copy. If it's unsaved, select File > Save As or Save a Copy.
  2. In the drop-down list, select PDF.
  3. Select More Options > Options to customize your PDF.
  4. To export only some pages, choose an option under Page range.

Page range is the quickest win for Word users: if a client only needs the pricing pages of a long proposal, export just those. Before exporting from any Office app, it also helps to delete slides, pages or images the recipient doesn't need.

Use a browser-based compressor

No Mac, no Acrobat, no source file? Browser-based compressors work through a website. The usual flow is the same across tools: open the tool, add your PDF, choose a compression level if one is offered, then download the smaller copy.

The trade-off is privacy. Freelancers and small business owners often handle contracts, invoices and client data. Before you put a client file into any online tool:

  • Read the tool's disclosure on where files are processed: in your browser, or on the company's servers.
  • Check whether and when uploaded files are deleted.
  • Avoid uploading contracts, ID documents or financial records unless you know how the tool handles them.
  • If you work under an NDA or for a company, check whether your agreement or policy allows third-party tools.

One option is Private PDF Editor, a browser-based PDF toolkit with about 30 tools, including compress, split and remove pages. Standard PDF editing runs in the browser; some conversions are handled differently and have separate disclosures, so check the disclosure for the specific tool you use. It has paid plans; see its pricing page for current prices.

Disclosure: Private PDF Editor is built by the team behind New Earns. We mention it where it fits the task; the steps in this guide also work with other tools.

Check the compressed file before you send it

A smaller file that looks blurry in front of a client costs more than it saves. Run through this before you hit send:

  • Open the compressed copy, not the original, and scroll through every page.
  • Zoom in on small text, footnotes and figures in tables. Invoices and contracts need to stay legible.
  • Check charts, photos and portfolio images for blockiness or washed-out colour.
  • Look at scanned signatures and stamps. These are often images and can degrade first.
  • Click the links to make sure they still work.
  • Test form fields if the recipient needs to fill something in.
  • Compare file sizes in Finder or File Explorer to confirm the copy is actually smaller and has headroom below your limit.
  • Keep the original. If you need to try a different setting, start again from the full-quality file rather than compressing an already compressed copy.

When compression isn't enough

Send a cloud link instead

For large portfolios, scanned documents or image-heavy reports, a link is often simpler than fighting the limit.

Gmail and Google Drive: Google says that if a file exceeds the Gmail size limit, you can attach it from Drive (Gmail Help: Send Google Drive attachments):

  1. At the top left, click Compose.
  2. At the bottom of the message, click Insert files using Drive.
  3. Select the files you want to attach.
  4. Choose how to send it: Drive link or Attachment. (Attachment only works for files that weren't created in Google Docs, Sheets, Slides or Forms; an uploaded PDF qualifies.)
  5. Click Insert.

Gmail checks whether your recipients have access to the file and prompts you to change the sharing settings if they don't. If recipients don't have a Google Account, use "Anyone with the link" sharing; Google's help page has the details. On work accounts, your admin may restrict which files you can share and with whom.

Outlook.com and OneDrive: Microsoft suggests sharing a file from OneDrive so recipients can receive larger files; the limit for OneDrive files is 2 GB (Microsoft Support).

Apple Mail and Mail Drop: Mail Drop lets you send attachments up to 5 GB. If a message is larger than your provider's limit, Mail will ask you to send the attachments using Mail Drop. Recipients have 30 days to download them, and Mail Drop works with a Mac running OS X Yosemite or later, iPhone or iPad with iOS 9.2 or later, and a Mac or PC with an updated browser (Apple Support: Mail Drop limits). Because the link expires, tell the recipient to download the file promptly.

Split the PDF across several emails

If the recipient needs real attachments, for example because their company blocks file-sharing links, split the PDF into parts that each fit comfortably under your provider's limit. Name them clearly, such as portfolio-part-1-of-3.pdf, and say in the first email how many messages to expect. Most PDF editors can split a file; the split tool in Private PDF Editor is one browser-based option.

Remove pages the recipient doesn't need

Often the easiest saving is content, not compression. Cover pages with full-bleed photos, appendices, duplicate scans and blank pages add size without helping the reader. Remove them in your PDF editor, or re-export only the pages you need from the source file using Page range in Word.

Troubleshooting common problems

Outlook says "The file you're attaching is bigger than the server allows"

This is the Outlook desktop message, which continues: "Try putting the file in a shared location and sending a link instead." Microsoft explains the cause is the 20 MB default for Internet email accounts in Outlook 2013 and later, or the maximum send size set by your Exchange administrator, which defaults to 10 MB (Microsoft Learn). Microsoft's documented fix for Internet accounts is a Windows registry change, which comes with a warning to back up the registry first; the Exchange limit can only be changed by an Exchange administrator. Unless you're comfortable editing the registry or you are the admin, compress the file further or share it via OneDrive instead.

Gmail replaced my attachment with a Drive link

That's expected behaviour. Google says that if your total attachment size is greater than the limit, 25 MB for personal accounts, Gmail automatically removes the attachment and adds it as a Google Drive link (Gmail Help). If you'd rather send a true attachment, compress the PDF until it fits, or split it.

The recipient can't open the Drive file

This is almost always a sharing setting. Open the file's sharing options in Google Drive and give the recipient access, or switch to "Anyone with the link" if they don't have a Google Account. Google's Drive attachments help page covers the options.

The file barely got smaller, or it looks blurry

Compression mostly works on images, so a text-only PDF may not shrink much, while an image-heavy one can lose sharpness. Try a different compression level, use Advanced Optimization if you have Acrobat Pro, re-export from the original Word or PowerPoint file, or split the document. Always restart from the original file rather than compressing the same copy repeatedly.

Gmail won't send any attachments at all

Check your Google storage. Google says attachments count toward your storage, which is shared across Drive, Photos and Gmail, and that if you reach your storage limit you can't send or receive attachments (Gmail Help). Free up space or add storage, then try again.

FAQ

What's the maximum PDF size I can attach in Gmail?

For personal Gmail accounts, the limit is 25 MB of total attachments per message; for work and school accounts, your administrator sets it (Gmail Help).

Why did my email bounce when the PDF was under the limit?

Encoding adds size in transit, and the recipient's service may have a lower limit. Microsoft documents a 33% encoding increase for messages leaving its datacenters (Exchange Online limits), and Apple notes the recipient's email app might have a smaller limit (Apple Support). Leave headroom or send a link.

Does compressing a PDF lower its quality?

It can. Apple warns that a compressed PDF may be of lower quality than the original, depending on the options you choose (Apple Support). Check the compressed copy before sending.

Can I compress a PDF without extra software?

Yes, in two common cases. On a Mac, use Preview's Reduce File Size Quartz filter (Apple Support). If you have the original PowerPoint file, export with Minimum size (publishing online) (Microsoft Support).
