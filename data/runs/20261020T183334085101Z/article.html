This guide is based on US federal rules and guidance from US agencies. It is general information, not financial or legal advice. Loan terms, state laws and licensing rules differ by state and by lender, so check the details that apply to you.

Applying for personal loans online can take only a few minutes, and that speed is the reason a careful process matters. The offer on your screen is only part of the picture. What a loan really costs depends on the APR, the fees, the repayment schedule, and whether the company behind the website is a licensed lender at all. This guide explains how to compare offers on equal terms, what to look for in the loan agreement, how to check that a lender is licensed, and how to recognize loan scams. It does not recommend or rank any lender.

What a Personal Installment Loan Is

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes a personal installment loan as one where you borrow a sum of money and pay it back in fixed amounts. These are closed-end loans: the lender gives you all of the money at the beginning, and you repay it over a set term. The CFPB notes that lenders usually consider factors such as your credit score, income, existing debts, and the loan amount and length when they set your rate and terms, and that personal loan rates can be fixed or adjustable.

A loan you apply for on a website works the same basic way as one from a bank branch. The difference is that the application, the disclosures and your signature usually all happen on a screen, often in one sitting. That puts the job of slowing down and checking the details on you.

Interest Rate vs. APR: What Each Number Includes

Loan offers usually show two rates, and they measure different things. The CFPB explains the difference: the interest rate is the cost you pay the lender for borrowing the money, while the annual percentage rate (APR) is a broader measure that adds the lender's additional fees to the interest rate. The CFPB says this includes origination charges and other fees charged when the loan is made.

Because the APR captures fees as well as interest, two loans with the same interest rate can have different APRs if one of them charges a larger up-front fee. When you compare offers, look at the APR for each one rather than the interest rate alone, and then check the dollar figures described in the next sections.

Origination fees and other charges

The CFPB lists fees that personal installment loans may carry. They include an origination fee, late fees, a documentation fee for processing paperwork, and add-on products such as credit insurance and disability insurance, which the CFPB describes as generally optional. If a loan is secured by collateral, non-filing insurance may also apply. The CFPB's advice is to check the lender's loan disclosure and loan documents to see which fees you may owe, and to shop around and compare offers from multiple lenders.

Optional add-ons deserve a close look. If an insurance product appears in your loan documents, ask whether you can decline it and what the loan would cost without it. Also ask how the origination fee is handled: whether it is subtracted from the money you receive or added to the amount you owe. Either way, it changes what you actually get compared with what you repay.

Total Cost of Credit: The Truth in Lending Disclosure

The federal Truth in Lending rules, known as Regulation Z, set out what lenders must disclose for closed-end consumer credit such as an installment loan. Section 1026.18 of Regulation Z requires the disclosure to identify the creditor and to include, among other items, the following terms:

  • Amount financed: described in the rule as "the amount of credit provided to you or on your behalf."
  • Finance charge: "the dollar amount the credit will cost you."
  • Annual percentage rate: "the cost of your credit as a yearly rate."
  • Payment schedule: the number, amounts and timing of the payments scheduled to repay the loan.
  • Total of payments: "the amount you will have paid when you have made all scheduled payments."
  • Late payment and prepayment terms: any charge that may be imposed for paying late, and whether a charge may apply if you pay off all or part of the principal early.

The finance charge and the total of payments turn rates into dollars, which makes them the easiest numbers to compare. A lower monthly payment spread over a longer term can still add up to a larger total of payments. A simple way to compare offers is to write down, for each one, the amount financed, the finance charge, the APR, the number of payments, the payment amount, and the total of payments. The offer with the smallest monthly payment is not always the cheapest one.

Prequalification vs. a Hard Credit Inquiry

Many online lenders offer to show you estimated rates before you formally apply. Before you start, ask what kind of credit check is involved. The CFPB explains credit inquiries this way: hard inquiries are often made by lenders after you apply for credit, and they can affect your credit score because most scoring models look at how recently and how frequently you apply. Soft inquiries, such as prescreening by prospective lenders or checking your own credit report, do not affect your credit scores.

Treat any estimated rate as a starting point. The terms that count are the ones in the final disclosure you receive before you sign, so compare that disclosure with the estimate you were shown.

Prescreened offers that arrive in the mail are a related but separate thing. The FTC says these offers typically come from information in your credit report and that prescreening doesn't hurt your credit score. If you don't want them, the FTC says you can opt out at optoutprescreen.com or by calling 1-888-5-OPT-OUT (1-888-567-8688).

Be cautious when the word "prequalified" comes from an unexpected phone call. The FTC warns that some phone scams start with a call saying you are prequalified for a loan, then ask for personal information such as your Social Security number or bank account number. Its advice is not to respond, not to call back, not to trust caller ID, and to block the number.

How to Read the Loan Agreement Before You Sign

The disclosure and the loan agreement are where the terms become binding. Before you sign, work through this list:

  • Check that the lender's legal name in the documents matches the company you looked up with your state regulator (covered in the next section).
  • Compare the APR, finance charge and total of payments with the estimate you were shown earlier.
  • Find every fee: origination, late, documentation, and any optional insurance or other add-on.
  • Read the prepayment terms so you know whether paying the loan off early could cost extra.
  • Note the payment due dates and how payments will be collected, especially if you are asked to authorize automatic withdrawals from your bank account.
  • Confirm whether the rate is fixed or adjustable, since the CFPB notes personal loan rates can be either.

If anything in the documents differs from what you were told, or you don't understand a term, ask the lender in writing before you sign. You can also save or print the disclosure so you have a record of what you agreed to.

How to Check That an Online Lender Is Licensed

The FTC states that lenders must register where they do business, and it recommends contacting your state attorney general or your state banking or financial services regulator to find out whether a lender is registered. The CFPB keeps a list of state banking regulators with contact details and complaint instructions for each state.

A second tool is NMLS Consumer Access, a free public website for looking up information about state-licensed companies. According to the NMLS information document about Consumer Access, records show each license's status and history by state, with an indicator of whether the company is authorized to conduct business, and state agencies post administrative or enforcement actions there.

The tool has limits. The same NMLS document notes that not every state agency uses NMLS as its system of record, so some licensees may not appear, and it points users to the state's own website for complete information about regulatory actions. If a lender doesn't show up on NMLS Consumer Access, confirm with your state regulator before drawing a conclusion. If it does show up, check that the license is for your state and that it is shown as authorized to do business.

Advance-Fee Loan Scams and Other Red Flags

An advance-fee loan scam promises you a loan, then asks you to pay before you receive any money. The FTC's guidance on advance-fee loans describes these warning signs:

  • A promise of approval before anyone checks your credit. The FTC says legitimate lenders will not promise you a loan without knowing your credit history. Real lenders check your credit report and confirm the information in your application.
  • Fees that are not disclosed clearly. The FTC notes that scammers don't disclose fees before you apply.
  • A payment demanded before the loan is granted. The FTC calls any up-front fee that a lender wants to collect before granting the loan a cue to walk away. It also notes that real lenders can require an application or appraisal fee before they consider an application, so ask exactly what any fee is for and check that it appears in the written disclosure.
  • Unusual payment methods. The FTC says scammers may ask you to pay with cryptocurrency, by wiring money through a company like MoneyGram or Western Union, or by putting money on a gift card and sharing the numbers on the back.
  • Telemarketing offers that require payment. The FTC says it is illegal for telemarketers to promise you a loan and ask you to pay for it up front.

Pressure is another warning sign. The FTC's alert on unexpected loan calls describes callers who claim an application is almost finished or urge you not to miss out. A real lender's offer can wait while you check its license and read its disclosure.

Where to Report a Loan Scam or Problem

  • The FTC. The FTC asks people to report advance-fee loan scams and unwanted loan calls at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • The CFPB. The CFPB's complaint portal covers personal loans such as installment, advance and title loans. The CFPB sends complaints to the company so it can review the issues, and lists a phone line at (855) 411-2372 for people who can't submit online.
  • Your state. Your state attorney general and your state banking or financial services regulator can also take complaints. The CFPB's state regulator list includes complaint instructions for each state.

Borrow Responsibly: Affordability and Missed Payments

Before you apply, work out whether the monthly payment fits your budget. The CFPB defines your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) as all your monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income. In the CFPB's example, $2,000 of monthly debt payments against a gross monthly income of $6,000 gives a DTI of 33 percent. A new loan payment raises that ratio, so calculate it with the new payment included and ask whether the rest of your budget still works.

Missed payments have real consequences. The CFPB notes that lenders may report your payments to one or more of the three major credit reporting companies, that failing to pay on time can have a significant impact on your credit scores and credit report, and that lenders might use third-party debt collectors. If you expect trouble making a payment, the CFPB suggests contacting your lender, which may offer options such as deferment, forbearance or a payment plan.

Borrow only what you need for a specific expense. Avoid borrowing to fund an investment or a business idea with uncertain results: the loan payments are due every month whether or not the idea works out.

Alternatives Worth Comparing First

Credit union loans. If you belong to a credit union, ask about its personal loans. For small, short-term needs, the National Credit Union Administration's consumer site explains that federal credit unions may offer Payday Alternative Loans (PALs). As of October 2026, that page describes PAL amounts of $200 to $1,000, terms of 1 to 6 months, an application fee of no more than $20, and a requirement that you have been a member for at least one month. It adds that many state-chartered credit unions offer similar products.

Nonprofit credit counseling. If a loan is meant to deal with existing debts, a credit counselor can help you look at the whole picture first. The CFPB says credit counseling organizations are usually nonprofit and can advise you on your money and debts, help you build a budget, and set up debt management plans. Counselors may charge fees for some services, so the CFPB suggests getting a specific price quote in writing. It names the Financial Counseling Association of America, the National Foundation for Credit Counseling, and the US Department of Justice's list of approved credit counseling agencies as places to start, and says a reputable organization should be willing to send you free information about its services without first asking for details about your situation.

Longer-term income. If the real problem is a recurring monthly shortfall rather than a one-time expense, extra income may help over time. Our guide on how to earn money from your phone covers some options. Side income takes time to build and is not a replacement for an emergency plan.

A Short Checklist Before You Apply

  1. Decide the exact amount you need and the monthly payment you can afford, including your debt-to-income ratio with the new payment.
  2. Check the lender with your state regulator and, where available, NMLS Consumer Access.
  3. Ask whether any rate check uses a soft or a hard credit inquiry.
  4. Collect offers and compare the APR, finance charge, fees and total of payments, not only the monthly payment.
  5. Read the full disclosure and agreement, including late payment, prepayment and add-on terms.
  6. Walk away from any lender that wants payment before granting the loan, asks for gift cards, wire transfers or cryptocurrency, or pressures you to decide on the spot.
  7. Report suspected scams to the FTC, the CFPB or your state.

Comparing personal loans online takes more effort than clicking the first approval button, but the steps above use free, official tools. Rules and agency guidance can change, so check the linked pages for current details before you borrow.
