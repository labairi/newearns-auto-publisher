Most lists of passive income ideas make the money sound automatic. In practice, every option on this page asks for something up front: savings you put at risk, or months of work building an asset before it pays. This guide walks through ten common passive income ideas, explains how each one actually earns, what it costs in time or money, and where the risks sit, with links to the regulators and platforms behind each fact.

Before you start: this is general information, not financial, investment, or tax advice. Investments can lose value, and the SEC's own beginner guide says that if you invest, you "could lose some or all of your money" (SEC: Beginners’ Guide to Asset Allocation, Diversification, and Rebalancing). Tax notes below describe US federal rules from IRS pages; rules in other countries, and in individual states, differ.

What "Passive" Really Means Here

It helps to sort these ideas into two groups. Investment income (dividends, interest, REIT payouts, loan repayments) needs capital and carries market or credit risk, but little ongoing labor once you have chosen what to buy. Asset-building income (a blog, a course, digital products, stock photos, a video channel) needs little money but a lot of upfront work, and usually some maintenance afterward. Neither group is effort-free, and neither produces a predictable amount.

The word "passive" also has a narrower tax meaning in the US. The IRS defines passive activities as trade or business activities "in which you don't materially participate," and says rental activities are generally passive even if you do participate (IRS Topic 425, Passive activities). That label affects how losses can be used. It does not make the income tax-free: in most cases the income from every idea below is taxable.

1. Dividend-Paying Stocks and Funds

How it works

Some companies pay part of their profits to shareholders as dividends. You can buy individual dividend-paying stocks through a brokerage account, or buy a fund that holds many of them. Payments depend on each company's results and its board's decisions, so the amount can change over time, and the share price itself rises and falls.

Risks and how to manage them

Stocks, the SEC notes, "have historically had the greatest risk and highest returns among the three major asset categories" (stocks, bonds, and cash). The same guide describes diversification, spreading money across different investments, as a way that "may be able to limit your losses" (SEC asset allocation guide). A fund that holds many companies is one simple way to avoid depending on a single company's payout.

Tax note (US)

The IRS treats taxable dividends as ordinary income, but "qualified dividends" are taxed at the lower capital gain rates; your broker reports which are which on Form 1099-DIV (IRS Topic 404, Dividends).

2. High-Yield Savings Accounts

How it works

Online banks and some credit unions pay higher interest on savings than many traditional accounts. This is the closest thing on the list to genuinely low-effort income: you open the account, deposit cash, and interest accrues. The trade-off is that the return is usually modest, so check the current rate and any conditions attached to it.

What protects your money

At an FDIC-insured bank, deposit insurance "covers $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category" (FDIC: Understanding Deposit Insurance). The FDIC also states it does not cover non-deposit investment products such as stocks, bonds, mutual funds, or crypto assets, even when a bank sells them. Before opening an account, confirm the institution is actually insured, not just partnered with an insured bank, and check whether the advertised rate is a promotional one.

Tax note (US)

Interest on bank accounts, money market accounts, and certificates of deposit is generally taxable, and you should receive Form 1099-INT when a payer pays you $10 or more (IRS Topic 403, Interest received).

3. Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)

How it works

Investor.gov describes a REIT as "a company that owns and typically operates income-producing real estate or related assets," which can include offices, apartments, warehouses, and mortgages (Investor.gov: Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)). REITs let you earn from property income without buying or managing a building yourself. According to the same page, you can buy shares of a publicly traded REIT through a broker, or buy a REIT mutual fund or REIT exchange-traded fund.

Publicly traded vs. non-traded REITs

The distinction matters. Publicly traded REITs are listed on an exchange, and the SEC's bulletin calls them "typically a liquid investment," though it also warns that "REIT investments may be sensitive to a changing interest rate environment" (Investor Bulletin: Publicly Traded REITs). Non-traded REITs are registered with the SEC but do not trade on an exchange. Investor.gov states that "Non-traded REITs are illiquid investments," that they generally have high up-front fees, that they typically do not give a per-share value estimate until 18 months after the offering closes, and that their distributions may be funded from offering proceeds and borrowings (Investor.gov REIT bulletin).

Checks before you buy

  • Confirm the REIT is registered with the SEC by searching the SEC's EDGAR database, as Investor.gov recommends; it also warns to be wary of anyone selling REITs that are not registered (Investor.gov REIT bulletin).
  • Check the background of the broker or adviser recommending it.
  • Ask whether there is any way to sell your shares early, and at what cost.

Tax note (US)

Investor.gov notes that REIT dividends "generally are treated as ordinary income" and do not get the reduced rate that applies to qualified corporate dividends (Investor.gov REIT bulletin).

4. Rental Property

How it works

Owning a rental home or unit can produce monthly rent and, possibly, long-term appreciation. It is also the least passive item here unless you pay someone else to run it: tenants need screening, repairs come up, and vacant months still carry a mortgage, insurance, and taxes. A property manager reduces your time commitment but takes part of the rent. Financing adds leverage, which magnifies both gains and losses.

Practical steps

  • Price out every cost before buying: financing, insurance, property tax, maintenance, vacancy, and management.
  • Keep a cash reserve for repairs and empty months.
  • Learn the landlord-tenant rules in your state and city before you sign a lease.

Tax note (US)

Rent you receive is taxable, and the IRS lists depreciation, repairs, and other operating expenses among the costs you can generally deduct; most real estate rentals are reported on Schedule E (IRS Topic 414, Rental income and expenses). Because rentals are generally passive activities, losses beyond your passive income may be limited and carried forward, with a limited exception for owners who actively participate (IRS Topic 425).

5. Peer-to-Peer Lending

How it works

Peer-to-peer (P2P) platforms let individuals fund loans to other people or small businesses and receive a share of the repayments with interest. It can look like a savings account with a better rate. It is not one.

The risks to understand

  • Borrower default. The North American Securities Administrators Association (NASAA) warns that P2P loans "are unsecured," that investors depend on the borrower to repay, and that they may have no legal ability to pursue a borrower who stops paying (NASAA: risks of peer-to-peer lending).
  • No deposit insurance. NASAA also notes that the notes "are not FDIC-insured," and no federal or state agency stands behind them (NASAA).
  • Illiquidity. In a 2019 prospectus filed with the SEC, Prosper Funding stated that "no trading platform for the transfer of Notes exists" and that investors "should be prepared to hold the Notes they purchase until maturity" (Prosper Funding prospectus, SEC EDGAR).
  • Platform risk. The same prospectus says that if the issuer went through bankruptcy, note holders' rights "may be uncertain." Platforms can also change their business: LendingClub, once a well-known P2P option, retired its retail Notes platform effective December 31, 2020 (The Motley Fool: LendingClub Is Ending Its P2P Lending Platform).

If you still want to try it

Treat P2P lending as a higher-risk slice of your money, not a cash substitute. Read the platform's current prospectus and risk disclosures, spread money across many small loans rather than a few large ones, and assume you may not be able to get out early. 6. A Niche Blog or Content Site

How it works

A blog earns through display ads, affiliate commissions, sponsorships, or your own products once it has steady readers. It usually takes many months of consistent publishing before traffic arrives, and older posts need updating to stay accurate. The "passive" part is that a useful article can keep earning long after it is written.

Steps to launch

  • Pick a topic you can write about for years, with a clear reader in mind.
  • Register a domain and choose hosting and a content platform.
  • Publish helpful, original articles on a regular schedule and update them as facts change.
  • Add an email list early, so you are not dependent on a single traffic source.

For the search side of this work, see our overview of SEO and digital marketing.

Disclose affiliate links

If you earn commissions through links, the FTC's guidance says to "disclose your relationship to the retailer clearly and conspicuously on your site," and that the closer the disclosure is to the recommendation, the better (FTC: Endorsement Guides, What People Are Asking).

7. Online Courses

How it works

If you have a skill people pay to learn, from spreadsheet modeling to watercolor, you can package it as a recorded course and sell it on a course marketplace or your own site. Marketplaces bring an audience but set much of the pricing and take a share; your own site keeps more of each sale but means you must find students yourself. Either way, course sales tend to follow the marketing you do, not just the quality of the videos.

How to build one

  • Validate demand first: ask your audience, or pre-sell a short version before recording everything.
  • Outline lessons around one concrete outcome for the student.
  • Record in short segments with clear audio; simple equipment is usually enough to start.
  • Collect questions from early students and update the course with what they missed.

AI tools can speed up outlining and editing; our guide on using artificial intelligence to start a business covers where they help and where they do not.

8. Digital Products

What counts

Ebooks, templates, planners, printables, presets, and stock graphics are made once and delivered as downloads, with no inventory or shipping. The ongoing work is customer support, updates, and marketing, since marketplaces hold many similar products.

Know the platform fees

Fees cut into every sale, so read each platform's own fee page. As an example, as of October 2026 Etsy's Fees & Payments Policy lists a $0.20 USD listing fee per item, listings that expire after four months, a transaction fee of 6.5% of the displayed price, and separate payment processing fees (Etsy Fees & Payments Policy). 9. Licensing Photos, Video, or Art

How it works

Stock agencies license your images, clips, or illustrations to buyers and pay you a share each time an item is licensed. Earnings per item can be small, so contributors typically build large, well-tagged portfolios over time.

Where to start

Shutterstock's contributor site describes it as a marketplace to "license your photos, videos, vectors, and more" and, as of October 2026, states that registration and submission are free (Shutterstock Contributor). Other agencies run similar programs; read each one's contributor terms and payout rules before uploading, and keep originals of everything you submit.

10. A YouTube Channel

How it works

Videos can earn from ads, channel memberships, sponsorships, and affiliate links. Evergreen tutorials and explainers tend to keep getting views longer than news-driven content. Still, growing a channel means publishing regularly, and new videos usually do more for a channel than old ones.

Monetization requirements

As of October 2026, YouTube's help center lists two ways to qualify for ad revenue in the YouTube Partner Program: 1,000 subscribers with 4,000 qualified public watch hours on long-form videos in the last 12 months, or 1,000 subscribers with 10 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days (YouTube Partner Program overview & eligibility). The same page requires living in a country or region where the program is available, having no active Community Guidelines strikes, linking one AdSense for YouTube account, and turning on 2-Step Verification. Taxes on Passive Income: The Short Version (US)

Income from these passive income ideas is generally taxable. A few federal rules come up often:

  • Creator income can trigger self-employment tax. If a blog, course, product shop, or channel is a business, you usually owe self-employment tax when net earnings are $400 or more; the IRS describes the rate as 12.4% for Social Security plus 2.9% for Medicare (IRS Topic 554, Self-employment tax).
  • Investment income can face an extra tax at higher incomes. The IRS applies a 3.8 percent Net Investment Income Tax to interest, dividends, capital gains, rental and royalty income above set income thresholds, for example $200,000 for single filers and $250,000 for married couples filing jointly (IRS: Net Investment Income Tax).
  • Rental losses may be limited under the passive activity rules described above (IRS Topic 425).

Keep records from the start, set aside money for taxes on income that has no withholding, and consider a qualified tax professional once the income becomes meaningful.

How to Choose Where to Start

  • Have savings but little time? Investment options such as insured savings, diversified dividend funds, or publicly traded REITs fit better, keeping in mind that only the savings account carries deposit insurance.
  • Have time and skills but little money? A blog, course, digital product, stock portfolio, or channel costs little to start but needs months of work.
  • Need the money soon? Avoid illiquid choices such as non-traded REITs and P2P notes, and keep an emergency fund in cash first.
  • Start with one idea, track what it earns against the time and money it takes, and add a second only when the first is running smoothly.

None of these approaches comes with a fixed return, and none is entirely hands-off. Chosen carefully, and with the risks understood, one or two can become a steady second income stream over time.
