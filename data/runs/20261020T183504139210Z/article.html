Plenty of people want to make money playing games, and a few routes really do pay. Most of them, though, pay slowly, unevenly, or only after a lot of unpaid work. This guide walks through seven realistic options, from streaming and video to testing, competition, and play-to-earn crypto games, with the current platform rules, the main risks, and the tax basics you should know before you start.

One number puts the industry in context. Newzoo estimates the global games market closed at $201.6 billion in 2025, as reported by GamesBeat. That money flows mostly to publishers, platforms, and hardware makers, not to individual players. A large industry creates openings, but it does not mean any single person can expect an income from it.

This article is general information, not financial, legal, or tax advice. Earnings from every method below vary widely, and many people earn nothing.

Before You Start: Set Realistic Expectations

Treat gaming income as a side project you build over months, not a quick payday. Three ideas help:

  • Skill is not enough on its own. Streaming and video reward entertainment, editing, and consistency as much as gameplay. Testing rewards careful written reporting. Competition rewards rare, top-tier ability.
  • Platforms set the rules. Thresholds, payout terms, and eligible countries change, so check the official help page before you plan around them.
  • Anything that asks you to pay first deserves suspicion. Legitimate work rarely requires a deposit, and regulators repeatedly warn about schemes that do.

1. Stream Your Gameplay Live

Live streaming earns money through viewer support (subscriptions, tips, and platform currencies), ads, and sponsorships. The catch is that almost every streamer starts with zero viewers, and growth usually depends on a regular schedule, a clear niche, and a personality people want to come back to.

Twitch

Twitch changed its rules in 2026. In its May 2026 "Monetization for All" announcement, Twitch said it was opening Bits, subs, emotes, badges, and Channel Points to all streamers, but it also stated that "streamers must earn Partner or Affiliate status to receive a payout." The same post lists updated Affiliate criteria: 25 or more followers, 4 hours of streaming on 4 different days, and a minimum of 3 average concurrent viewers on 4 different days (previously 50 followers, 8 hours, and 7 days). The post is dated May 13, 2026 and describes the criteria change as happening "in early June," so confirm the current numbers in your Creator Dashboard. These details are as of October 2026.

YouTube

YouTube works for both live streams and uploaded videos. As of October 2026, the YouTube Partner Program help page lists two routes to join: 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 qualified watch hours on public long-form videos in the last 12 months, or 1,000 subscribers plus 10 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days. You also need to follow the channel monetization policies, live in a country where the program is available, have no active Community Guidelines strikes, turn on 2-Step Verification, and link an AdSense for YouTube account. Meeting the thresholds starts a review; it does not guarantee approval. The page also notes that updated program terms apply from February 1, 2027, and creators should accept them in YouTube Studio by January 31, 2027.

Getting set up

You do not need expensive software to begin. OBS Studio describes itself as "free and open source software for video recording and live streaming," and it works with both Twitch and YouTube. A clear microphone usually matters more to viewers than a high-end camera.

2. Make Gaming Videos and Short Clips

Recorded content is often a better fit than live streaming if you prefer editing to performing, or if your schedule is unpredictable. Videos keep getting found long after you upload them, while a live stream mostly earns while you are online.

Formats that tend to work well for gaming channels include:

  • Guides and walkthroughs that answer a specific question, such as how to beat a boss or build a character.
  • Highlights and edited clips cut from longer sessions, which also suit Shorts and other vertical video feeds.
  • Reviews and first impressions of new releases or updates.
  • Challenge runs or series that give viewers a reason to subscribe for the next episode.

Once you qualify for the YouTube Partner Program described above, income can come from ads and fan features such as memberships and Supers. Many creators also earn from sponsorships and affiliate links. If you take sponsored deals, label them clearly so viewers know what is paid.

3. Work as a Game Tester

Game testing, often called quality assurance (QA), means playing builds of a game to find and document bugs. It is real work rather than casual play: you may repeat the same level dozens of times and write precise reports on exactly how to reproduce each problem.

  • Studio careers pages are the most direct source of QA roles, including entry-level and contract positions.
  • Freelance marketplaces sometimes list short testing or feedback gigs, mostly from smaller developers.
  • Public betas and playtests are usually unpaid, but they let you practice writing bug reports you can show employers.

Be careful with "tester" offers that arrive by text or messaging apps, or that ask you to pay before you can start. The FTC's guidance on how to spot and avoid task scams advises people to ignore unsolicited job offers sent that way and to never pay money to start a job or to get paid. In the scams it describes, the "earnings" shown on screen are fake, and victims are told to deposit their own money, usually in crypto, to unlock them.

4. Compete in Esports Tournaments

Competitive play pays through prize money, team contracts, and sponsorships, but the large prize pools you hear about go to a very small group of top professionals. For most players, local and online amateur events are a way to build skill and a reputation rather than a reliable income.

Where you compete has changed recently. ESL Play, which the old version of this guide recommended, has shut down; its closure notice says its competitions moved to FACEIT. FACEIT describes itself as the place "where competitive players grind matchmaking or team competitions." It is also worth checking whether the games you play have official competitive modes or qualifiers.

Practical steps if you want to try:

  • Pick one game and one role, and review your own matches to find mistakes.
  • Join a team or community so you can enter team events and get noticed.
  • Read each tournament's rules on entry fees, age limits, regions, and how prizes are paid before you register.

5. Play-to-Earn and Crypto Games: Understand the Risks First

Play-to-earn (P2E) games reward players with crypto tokens or NFTs that can sometimes be sold. This is the riskiest route in this guide, because what you earn is a speculative asset whose price can collapse, and because the space attracts fraud. Many of these games also require you to buy tokens or NFTs before you can play, which turns a "job" into an investment.

What regulators warn about

  • Volatility and total loss. The SEC's investor alert on crypto asset securities calls these investments "exceptionally volatile and speculative" and advises that the only money you should put at risk with any speculative investment is money you can afford to lose entirely.
  • No safety net. The FTC's page on cryptocurrency and scams says crypto held in accounts is not insured by a government, its value "can change rapidly, even changing by the hour," and payments "typically are not reversible." It also warns that "only scammers will guarantee profits or big returns."
  • Hype-driven schemes. The CFTC's page on digital asset frauds describes pump-and-dump scammers who hype little-known tokens on social media and messaging apps, then sell and leave others with losses. It also warns that there is no such thing as a risk-free transaction.
  • Rug pulls. A rug pull is when insiders drain the money backing a token and leave holders with something nearly worthless. In a January 2025 case involving a token called "Game Coin," the SEC alleged that a coder kept supposedly "locked" liquidity unlocked and withdrew about $553,000 in crypto assets, behavior the SEC said is "commonly known in the crypto asset industry as a 'rug pull.'"

Questions to ask before you join

  • Do I have to buy a token or NFT before I can earn anything? If so, how much could I lose?
  • Where does the money paid to players come from? If it depends mostly on new players buying in, the rewards may not last.
  • Has the token's price history been stable or sharply up and down? A price tracker such as CoinGecko shows historical charts, but past prices tell you nothing about future ones.
  • Is anyone promising fixed returns or profits that cannot fail? Regulators say that is a warning sign in itself.

6. Reward Apps and Mobile Games That Pay

Some mobile apps pay small rewards, such as gift cards or in-app currency, for playing games, reaching levels, or completing offers. These can work as a little extra spending money, but the payouts are typically small for the time involved, and reward offers do not always deliver.

That last point is not hypothetical. In January 2021 the FTC announced a proposed settlement with Tapjoy, a mobile advertising company, after alleging that it failed to provide promised in-game rewards "in many instances" even after people completed the required activities, and that it received "hundreds of thousands of complaints from consumers."

To protect your time and money:

  • Read the app's terms on minimum cash-out amounts, expiry, and eligible countries before investing hours.
  • Avoid apps or "games" that ask for a deposit, crypto, or a fee to unlock withdrawals; the FTC's task-scam guidance linked above describes exactly that pattern.
  • Be cautious about installing offers that ask for unusual permissions or personal data.
  • For more ideas beyond games, see our guide on how to earn money from your phone.

7. Sell Items or Accounts: Read the Terms of Service

The original version of this guide suggested selling rare items or high-level accounts. Before doing that, check the rules of the game and platform, because many prohibit it outright, and breaking them can get the account banned with no refund.

Two examples, as of October 2026:

  • Riot Games (League of Legends, Valorant): its Terms of Service say "You can't sell, transfer or allow any other person to access your account or Login Credentials, or offer to do so," and that you can't redeem virtual content "for any type of 'real world' money."
  • Steam: the Steam Subscriber Agreement says you may not "sell or charge others for the right to use your Account, or otherwise transfer your Account," and that "Steam Wallet funds have no cash value and are not exchangeable for cash." In other words, a Steam Wallet balance, however you build it, cannot be cashed out.

Rules differ by game, and some titles allow trading inside an official marketplace. If a game's terms do not clearly permit a sale, assume it is not allowed. Deals made through unofficial third-party sites can also leave you with little recourse if the other side does not hold up its end.

Taxes on Gaming Income

Money from games is generally taxable. In the US, the IRS gig economy tax center says you must report income from the gig economy even if it is not reported on a form such as a 1099-K, 1099-NEC, or W-2. Its examples include providing creative or professional services and selling goods online, which can cover streaming, sponsorships, freelance testing, and item sales.

Crypto rewards count too. The IRS digital assets page states that "income from digital assets is taxable," lists NFTs among its examples of digital assets, and says digital asset transactions must be reported whether or not they result in a gain or loss. Keep records of what you earned, when, and its value at the time. Tax rules for prize money, business expenses, and other countries vary, so check with a qualified tax professional.

How to Choose a Starting Point

The right route depends on what you enjoy and how much risk you can accept:

  • Like talking and entertaining? Try live streaming, and clip your best moments into short videos.
  • Prefer editing and planning? Focus on guides and video series.
  • Detail-oriented and want steady pay? Look for QA and testing roles.
  • Highly competitive? Enter amateur tournaments and treat winnings as a bonus.
  • Curious about crypto games? Only use money you can afford to lose, and read the regulator warnings above first.

Whatever you choose, start with one method, track your hours against what you earn, and keep your gaming fun. If you want to turn a hobby into a more structured side business, our guide to turning a passion into profit covers the next steps.
