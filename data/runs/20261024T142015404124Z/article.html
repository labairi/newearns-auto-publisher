This guide gives you copy-ready ChatGPT prompts for the work freelancers and side-business owners do every week: proposals, client emails, payment reminders, content repurposing, product listings and spending checks. Each template tells you what to paste in and what to check before anything reaches a client. You also get the four-part structure OpenAI recommends, so you can adapt any prompt instead of collecting hundreds of them.

The templates are editorial examples written for this guide, not proven formulas. Swap in your own details, run them, and edit the output.

What a good prompt includes

Goal, Context, Output, Boundaries

OpenAI's ChatGPT Learn prompting guide says a short prompt is often enough, but for larger or more important tasks you should include the parts that matter:

  • Goal: what ChatGPT should do.
  • Context: the information or sources that will help.
  • Output: the format, length or level of detail you need.
  • Boundaries: what must stay unchanged, and what ChatGPT should avoid or check with you before it acts.

The same guide says to use only the parts that help; you don't need to fill in every item or follow a required format. It also recommends starting with the result you want rather than a detailed list of steps, and naming the audience or format when those details change what ChatGPT should produce.

Same idea, different vendor

Google's Gemini for Workspace prompt guide names four areas: Persona, Task, Context and Format, and notes that you don't need all four in every prompt. The labels differ, but the principle is the same: say what you want, give the background, and describe the shape of the answer.

Your first prompt doesn't have to be perfect

OpenAI's help center describes prompting as iterative: start with a prompt, review the response, then adjust the wording, add context or simplify the request. ChatGPT Learn puts it simply: review the result, then ask for the specific change you want. Follow-ups such as “Make it shorter” or “Put the price in the first line” are part of the process.

Prompts for finding and scoping client work

These templates assume you already have a lead or a job post. If you're still deciding where to find work, start with How to Make Money Online: 5 Ways and What Each Platform Takes. In every template, replace the square-bracket parts with your own details.

Draft a proposal from a job post

What to paste in: the full job post, two or three examples of relevant work, and your availability.

Prompt: Goal: Draft a proposal for the job post below. Context: The post is under JOB POST; my relevant experience is under MY EXPERIENCE. Output: Under 200 words. Open with a line that refers to a specific detail in the post, then explain how I'd approach the work, then end with one question for the client. Boundaries: Use only the experience I list. Don't invent experience, results, clients or statistics. If the post asks for something my experience doesn't cover, flag it instead of filling the gap.

Check before you use it: every claim about you is true, and the “specific detail” actually appears in the post.

Questions to ask before quoting a project

What to paste in: the client's first message or project description, and a line on what you normally deliver.

Prompt: I'm a [freelance web designer] preparing a quote for the project below. List the questions I should ask before I can price it, grouped under scope, timeline, budget, approvals and materials the client will supply. Put the five most important questions first. Keep each question to one plain-English sentence.

Check before you use it: delete questions the brief already answers and add anything specific to your service.

Turn a messy brief into a scope-of-work outline

What to paste in: the brief, relevant emails and your call notes.

Prompt: Turn the notes below into a scope-of-work outline with these headings: objective, deliverables, out of scope, client responsibilities, timeline, revision rounds, open questions. Use only what's in the notes. Where the notes don't cover something, write “To confirm with client” instead of guessing. Keep the approved dates and budget figures unchanged.

Check before you use it: compare every line with the original notes. A scope you send reads as a commitment. If you're growing freelance work into an agency, see How to Start Your Digital Marketing Agency in 2025.

Prompts for client communication

Meeting notes to follow-up email

This one is adapted from an example in OpenAI's prompting guide, which also suggests asking for a final check on important work.

What to paste in: your raw meeting notes.

Prompt: Turn these notes into a follow-up email with the decisions, owners and due dates. Put the decisions first. Before the email, confirm that every action item has an owner and a due date, and list any that don't. Use only the supplied notes. Flag missing information instead of guessing.

Check before you use it: names, dates and who agreed to what.

Weekly status update with decisions first

What to paste in: your notes from the week.

Prompt: Turn these notes into a short weekly update for [client name]. Put decisions and next steps first, then progress, then anything I need from the client. Under 150 words, friendly and professional. Keep all dates and figures unchanged.

Check before you use it: nothing is promised that you haven't agreed to.

Push back on scope creep

Adapted from OpenAI's example of declining an invitation in under 120 words.

What to paste in: the client's new request and the relevant line from your agreed scope.

Prompt: Draft a friendly email declining this extra request because it's outside the agreed scope (pasted below). Keep it under 120 words, offer to quote it as a separate piece of work, and leave the door open. Apologise no more than once.

Check before you use it: the scope line you quote matches your actual agreement.

A friendly late-payment reminder

What to paste in: invoice number, amount, due date and how the client can pay.

Prompt: Write a friendly reminder that invoice [number] for [amount], due [date], hasn't been paid. Include how to pay: [details]. Under 100 words, polite and direct. Keep the invoice number, amount and dates exactly as given. Prepare the message as a draft. Don't send it.

Check before you use it: every number against your invoice. “Prepare the message as a draft. Don't send it” is one of the boundary examples OpenAI lists, and a sensible habit for anything involving money.

Fix the tone of a rough reply

OpenAI's help center says descriptive adjectives such as formal, friendly or professional help guide tone.

Prompt: Rewrite my reply below in a friendly, professional tone. Keep every fact, date and price the same. Make it shorter if you can without dropping information.

Check before you use it: it still sounds like you.

Prompts for content and creator work

Repurpose one long post into short social posts

What to paste in: the full article, the platform and your audience.

Prompt: Turn the article below into five short posts for [platform] aimed at [audience]. Each post makes one point from the article and stands alone. Output a numbered list. Use only ideas and facts from the article; don't add statistics, quotes or claims that aren't in it.

Check before you use it: the platform's current length limits, and that each post matches the article.

Headline and hook options

Prompt: Give me 10 headline options and 5 opening hooks for the post below, aimed at [audience]. Mix direct, curiosity-led and benefit-led styles. Don't add statistics, numbers, studies or quotes that aren't in the post.

Check before you use it: the “no statistics” line matters. OpenAI says ChatGPT can hallucinate, including fabricated quotes, studies, citations or references to non-existent sources. A punchy invented number in a headline is exactly that risk.

Explain a topic for beginners

Adapted from OpenAI's compound-interest example.

Prompt: Explain [topic] for someone who has never [done it]. Use one concrete example and define any terms you introduce. Under 300 words.

Check before you use it: technical details against a reliable source before you publish.

Product description from your own notes

What to paste in: your product notes, specs and who it's for.

Prompt: Write a product description for [product] for [buyer]. Use only the features and specs listed below. Output: a one-sentence summary, three bullet points on benefits tied to listed features, then the specs as a list. Don't invent features, materials, certifications, measurements or results.

Check before you use it: every feature and number against your notes.

FAQ draft from real customer questions

What to paste in: real customer questions with names and contact details removed, plus your shipping, refund or delivery policies.

Prompt: Group the customer questions below into an FAQ of no more than eight questions. Answer each using only the policy text I've pasted. If the policies don't answer a question, write “Needs answer from owner” instead of guessing.

Check before you use it: each answer matches your current policy.

Planned vs. actual spending table

This is close to an example in OpenAI's guide.

Prompt: Create a clear table of planned versus actual spending from the figures below and highlight any difference over 10%. Add a total row and list any line where a figure is missing. Use only the figures I've pasted.

Check before you use it: recalculate totals in your spreadsheet. OpenAI advises users to always verify data.

Save your best prompts so you don't retype them

Once a few ChatGPT prompts work for you, store the parts you reuse. Menu names may differ by app version and plan.

Custom instructions for preferences in every chat

OpenAI recommends putting preferences that should apply across chats in custom instructions and keeping details for one chat in the prompt. According to the custom instructions help page, they're available on all plans on web, desktop, iOS and Android:

  • Web and desktop: Settings, then Personalization.
  • iOS and Android: Settings, then Customize ChatGPT; make sure Enable customization is on, then use the Custom Instructions field.
  • Limits: Free and Go users can save up to 1,500 characters; Plus, Pro, Enterprise, Business and Education users up to 5,000 (source).

An example: “I'm a freelance copywriter. Use British English. Keep emails short. Never invent client names, results or statistics.”

Projects for each client or side business

Projects keep related chats, files and instructions together, and are available to signed-in users subject to plan and workspace settings. To set one up:

  1. Select New project in the sidebar, enter a name, and pick an icon and color.
  2. Upload PDFs, spreadsheets, docs or images, or paste text, such as a client's brief and style guide.
  3. Open the more options menu (•••), select Project settings, and add instructions. These apply only inside that project and override your global custom instructions.
  4. Drag existing chats onto the project or use Move to project. Chats created with a GPT can't be moved in.

Per OpenAI, you can create unlimited projects, upload only 10 files at a time, and store 5 files per project on Free, 25 on Go and Plus, and 40 on Edu, Pro, Business and Enterprise (source).

Keep client information private

According to OpenAI's data controls page, data sharing is on by default for personal Free, Plus and Pro workspaces. You can turn off Improve the model for everyone and keep your chat history. On web, open Settings, select Data controls, select Improve the model for everyone, turn it off and select Done. On mobile, select your profile icon to open Settings and turn it off. Once it's off, new conversations won't be used to train OpenAI models but still appear in your history; it doesn't delete saved chats. Voice mode has a separate control.

The same page says temporary chats don't appear in history, don't create or update memories, aren't used to improve models, and may be retained for up to 30 days for safety. If you save a temporary chat, it becomes a regular chat that follows your account settings.

Two caveats from OpenAI:

  • If you give feedback, such as thumbs up or down, the entire conversation tied to that feedback may be used for training.
  • Information from your use of custom instructions is also used to improve model performance; the page links to opt-out information.

By default, OpenAI does not use content from Business, Enterprise, Edu or ChatGPT for Healthcare workspaces to train its models. Whatever your plan, it's good practice to remove names, emails and account details before pasting, and to check what your client contract says about third-party tools.

Check everything before it reaches a client

OpenAI says ChatGPT can produce incorrect or misleading outputs and may sound confident when wrong. It recommends using it as a first draft, not a final source, and always verifying quotes, data, technical information and references.

  • When an answer depends on current information, ask ChatGPT to use web search and ask for sources. Search access depends on your plan, per OpenAI.
  • For important work, ask for a final check, such as flagging information it couldn't verify, then review it yourself.
  • Don't ask ChatGPT whether a text was AI-written. OpenAI says those answers are not reliable evidence of authorship.

Don't use ChatGPT to write fake reviews

In the US, the FTC announced a final rule on fake reviews in August 2024. It addresses reviews and similar customer endorsements that misrepresent that they are by someone who does not exist, such as AI-generated fake reviews, or by someone who had no actual experience with the business or its products, or that misrepresent the person's experience. It prohibits businesses from creating or selling them, and covers buying or disseminating them when the business knew or should have known they were fake. The FTC said the rule lets it seek civil penalties against knowing violators. Use the FAQ template above with real customer questions instead.

What research says about time saved (and where AI makes work worse)

In a preregistered experiment published in Science in 2023, Noy and Zhang gave occupation-specific, midlevel writing tasks to 453 college-educated professionals; those given ChatGPT took 40% less time on average and produced 18% higher-quality output (abstract).

A September 2023 Harvard Business School working paper by Dell'Acqua and colleagues, still in draft form, studied 758 consultants. On 18 realistic tasks inside AI's capabilities, those using GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster and produced more than 40% higher-quality results than a control group. On one task chosen to be outside those capabilities, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions (paper).

The takeaway: these were specific tasks and groups, not a promise for your work. Try a prompt on your own task, check the output, and judge the time saved yourself.

Wrapping up

Pick the two or three templates closest to your weekly work, fill in the brackets, and keep the boundary lines. Save what you reuse in custom instructions or a project, protect client details, and check every number and claim before you hit send.

FAQ

Do I need all four parts in every prompt?

No. OpenAI says to use only the parts that help, and Google's guide says the same about its four areas.

Will client information I paste be used to train models?

On personal Free, Plus and Pro workspaces, data sharing is on by default, but you can turn off Improve the model for everyone or use a temporary chat, per OpenAI's data controls page. Feedback ratings are an exception.

Can ChatGPT tell me if a client's text was written by AI?

Not reliably. OpenAI says ChatGPT can't reliably determine whether text was AI-generated.
