Most lists of remote side hustles treat “remote” and “from anywhere” as the same thing. They aren't. Remote means the work happens online. Whether you can do it from another country depends on three things the lists usually skip: the platform's country rules, how and where it pays you, and the immigration and tax rules of the place where you're physically working.

This guide checks a short list of options against those three tests, using each platform's own pages. It doesn't promise income. What you earn depends on your skills, your hours and demand on each platform. If you'd rather have a remote employer than run gigs, see Remote Jobs with No Experience: Where to Find Real Ones. If your limit is time at home rather than travel, Side Hustles for Stay-at-Home Moms sorts ideas by the hours you have.

Side hustles that move with you

Freelance services for direct clients

Writing, editing, design and virtual assistance are skills, not platforms, so the portability question changes. If you find clients directly and invoice them yourself, there's no platform country list to check. You still need a way to receive payment in your country of residence, and the visa and tax rules later in this guide still apply wherever you sit down to work.

If you find clients through a freelance marketplace, that marketplace's eligibility rules, fees and payout options sit on top of all that. Fee structures change often, so we cover them in one place: How to Make Money Online: 5 Ways and What Each Platform Takes. For pitches, proposals and client emails, ChatGPT Prompts for Freelancers and Side Hustles has templates you can adapt.

A few habits make direct-client work easier to take with you:

  • Agree in writing which currency you invoice in and who pays any transfer costs.
  • Keep one payment account tied to your country of residence rather than opening new ones as you travel.
  • State your working hours in your client's time zone, and update them when you move.

Online tutoring

Preply's teach page says “No specific certification or teaching experience is required!” Sign-up runs roughly like this, though the screens may differ from what's described here:

  1. Create your tutor profile and add a photo.
  2. Record a short video introduction, up to 2 minutes long.
  3. Set your availability and your hourly rate.
  4. Wait for review. Preply says its Tutor Success team reviews profiles within 3 business days.

You choose your hourly rate and can change it anytime. As a reference point, Preply says English tutors charge $15–25 per hour on average. That's what tutors charge, not what they keep: Preply takes 100% of a trial lesson with a new student, then 33% of later lessons, falling to 18% as your teaching hours on the platform grow.

Why it travels: Preply pays tutors through PayPal, Payoneer, Skrill or Transferwise, and says students come from over 180 countries. The catch is your setup. Preply lists a laptop or desktop, a stable internet connection, a webcam and a microphone, so check the connection wherever you plan to teach. If you change time zones, change your availability too, so students don't book lessons at 3 a.m. your time.

Selling digital products: Gumroad and Etsy

Gumroad's pricing page charges per transaction, and the rate depends on how the buyer found you: 10% + $0.50 for sales through your profile or direct links, and 30% when new customers find and buy from you through its Discover marketplace. It says that since January 1, 2025, Gumroad handles your tax obligations as merchant of record, with a condition: “We collect taxes in regions where we have tax obligations as a merchant of record. This varies by location.” Payouts go by direct deposit or PayPal, and which one you get varies by country.

Etsy's Fees & Payments Policy says you may have to pay a one-time set-up fee to open a shop. After that, you pay $0.20 per listing (listings expire after four months) and a 6.5% transaction fee on the displayed price plus whatever you charge for shipping and gift wrapping. Two rules matter if you move around. Payment processing fees vary with the location of your bank account. And if you list in a currency other than your payment account's, Etsy charges a 2.5% currency conversion fee. Etsy lists all its fees exclusive of VAT.

Neither platform is the right pick for everyone. Gumroad's rate changes depending on whether the sale came from your own link or its marketplace, while Etsy charges per listing as well as per sale. For working from anywhere, ask both the same questions: which payout methods work in your country of residence, and what currency or processing costs apply to your bank account. If you'd like to sell physical products without holding stock, Print on Demand for Beginners covers a related model.

Usability testing

On UserTesting's contributor page, joining works like this (the flow may change):

  1. Submit your email and take a quick practice test to join its “global network of trusted contributors.” You'll need a microphone for the practice test.
  2. If you apply on a phone, install the UserTesting app.
  3. Answer screening questions. These decide which tests you're matched with, so you won't qualify for every test.
  4. Complete tests. Rewards vary by test type, duration and customer demand, and each amount is shown per test. UserTesting says payments are usually sent 14 days later.

Because tests involve speaking your thoughts aloud, a quiet room and a working microphone matter more than where you are. If you want other small, task-based online work, Data Entry Jobs from Home covers where to find real listings and how to spot scams.

Side hustles tied to where you live

Paid research studies on Prolific

Prolific shows clearly how a side hustle can be tied to your address. Its eligibility page says it is open to participants from most OECD countries, as long as you're over 18 and have a verified account. The list includes the US, UK, Canada, Australia, Japan and many European countries, plus countries such as India, Brazil, Egypt, Indonesia and South Africa. Check the full list on that page. Eligibility “is based on where you currently live, not your nationality.”

If your country isn't supported, the same page says you can join a waitlist, but Prolific says it can't promise access. In some cases it invites people from unsupported countries, who then need to submit a valid photo ID, ideally a passport. Prolific's participants page says you can “Get verified in 15 minutes.”

Before you rely on it while travelling, know these rules:

  • Your phone number: it will usually need to match your country of residence. US and Canada residents need a US or Canadian number, and UK and Ireland residents need a UK or Irish number. Everyone else needs a number from the same continent, from a country on the accepted list.
  • Studies abroad: taking part in studies while visiting a country other than your registered residence, or using a VPN, “will most likely result in your account being placed on hold.”
  • Cashing out abroad: “You must be in your listed country of residence to cash out.” If you try from abroad, you'll likely see a PEC-U-0001 error. Prolific's advice is to wait until you're back home.
  • VPN detection: Prolific uses systems to detect VPNs, proxies and private relays. Public, workplace or university Wi-Fi and iCloud Private Relay can also trigger it, which matters if you work from cafés or coworking spaces.
  • Cash-out timing: you can cash out once your combined GBP and USD balance reaches £6/$6, generally once a day (resetting at midnight UTC). A 72-hour cool-off applies if you recently added or updated your PayPal address, or have made fewer than four cash-outs.
  • Moving permanently: you can keep your account if your new country is supported by Prolific, PayPal and Hyperwallet. Your PayPal account must match your new country of residence, and you may be asked to reverify your details.

How to check any platform before you sign up

Prolific publishes its rules in detail. Many platforms don't put them on their landing pages, so look for them before you spend time on onboarding:

  1. Find the eligibility or supported-countries page in the help centre. Note whether it's based on residence, nationality or both.
  2. Find the payout methods page and check that at least one method works in your country of residence.
  3. Search the help centre for “VPN”, “travel”, “abroad” or “moving countries” to see what happens to your account if you log in from elsewhere.
  4. Check whether identity or phone verification has to match your country.
  5. Read the fee page yourself rather than relying on a list article, including this one. Fees change.

Before you work from another country: visas and taxes

Visitor visas aren't work permits

Being able to log in from another country doesn't mean you're allowed to work there. The UK makes a useful worked example because its rules are published online, and two official texts need to be read side by side:

  • The GOV.UK overview for the Standard Visitor route says you can usually stay for up to 6 months. It also says you cannot “do paid or unpaid work for a UK company or as a self-employed person, unless you're doing a permitted paid engagement or event.” The business visitor page adds: “You'll need to apply for a work visa if you want to do any other paid or unpaid work in the UK.”
  • The Immigration Rules, in Appendix Visitor: Permitted Activities, paragraph PA 4(h), say a visitor may “undertake activities relating to their employment overseas remotely from within the UK, providing this is not the primary purpose of their visit.”

This guide doesn't decide which text covers your situation. The overview mentions self-employed work, and the rule refers to “employment overseas.” If you plan to freelance or run a side hustle while visiting the UK, read both pages in full. If your case is still unclear, check with official UK government guidance or a qualified immigration adviser.

Then repeat the check for every country you plan to work from. Use that country's official immigration website, not a travel blog, and don't assume its rules match the UK's.

Taxes follow you

Moving doesn't switch off your tax obligations at home. For US readers, the IRS spells out the basics:

  • US citizens and resident aliens living abroad “are subject to tax on worldwide income from all sources.”
  • The foreign earned income exclusion and the foreign tax credit can reduce what you owe, but the IRS says you can only get them by filing a relevant US return.
  • If you qualify, you're allowed an automatic 2-month filing extension, to June 15. But you will still pay interest on any tax not paid by the regular due date. The same page covers reporting certain foreign financial accounts (FBAR) above the reporting threshold.
  • Self-employment tax is 15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. You must pay it and file Schedule SE if your net earnings from self-employment were $400 or more. For 2026, the Social Security part applies to the first $184,500 of combined wages, tips and net earnings.
  • The IRS notes you may have to pay estimated taxes quarterly rather than all at once.

If you're unsure how a given income stream is classified, Passive Income vs Active Income explains what the IRS actually calls “passive.” Outside the US, check your own tax authority's guidance on tax residence and on declaring self-employed or online income. Then check whether the country you're working from has rules of its own.

How to start one this week

  1. Pick by what you already have. A webcam, a mic and a subject you can teach point to tutoring. Files you can sell point to digital products. A mic and patience point to usability testing. If you live in a supported country, paid studies are an option.
  2. Read the country rules first. Open the platform's eligibility page and confirm your country of residence is covered.
  3. Set up the payout account the platform needs (PayPal, Payoneer, direct deposit and so on) in your country of residence, with matching details.
  4. Do the onboarding. That could mean a Preply profile and intro video, the UserTesting practice test, or the Prolific waitlist and identity check. Expect screens to differ from any guide, this one included.
  5. Check your travel plans against both the platform's rules and the immigration rules of where you'll be.
  6. Track earnings and fees from day one in a simple spreadsheet, so tax time isn't guesswork.

FAQ

Can I use a VPN to join a platform from an unsupported country?

On Prolific, no. Using a VPN “will most likely result in your account being placed on hold,” and Prolific uses systems to detect VPNs, proxies and private relays. If your country isn't supported, the official route is its waitlist. For other platforms, search their help centre for “VPN” before you try.

Can I get paid while travelling?

It depends on the platform. Prolific says you must be in your listed country of residence to cash out. Preply and Gumroad publish payout methods on their pages, but check each platform's rules on logging in from abroad.

Do I pay tax on a small side hustle?

In the US, you must pay self-employment tax and file Schedule SE if your net earnings from self-employment were $400 or more. Elsewhere, check your own tax authority's rules.

Does “remote” mean I can work on a tourist visa?

Not automatically. In the UK, the Standard Visitor overview and paragraph PA 4(h) of the Immigration Rules both cover work during a visit, and you should read them for your own case. For any other country, check its official immigration website before you work there.
